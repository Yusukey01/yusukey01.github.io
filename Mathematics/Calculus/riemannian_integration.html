Recovering the Integral of a Function

The theory of integration on manifolds was built around forms of top degree, not functions, for a reason that was unavoidable. On a bare smooth manifold a real-valued function has no coordinate-independent integral. Integrating its coordinate expression in a different chart introduces, through the change-of-variables formula, a Jacobian factor that the function alone cannot absorb, so the number one would compute depends on the chart, which is no number at all. A top-degree form is exactly the object engineered to absorb that factor, and that is why integration was defined for forms rather than functions.

A Riemannian metric removes the obstruction. On an oriented manifold the metric singles out a canonical top-degree form, the Riemannian volume form \(\omega_g\). Its coordinate expression carries precisely the factor \(\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\) needed to make the resulting integral coordinate-independent. Given such a form, a function can be turned into a form simply by multiplication, and then integrated. This section makes that precise and records the one positivity property that every later estimate rests on.

The Definition

Let \((M, g)\) be an oriented Riemannian manifold with or without boundary, and let \(\omega_g\) denote its Riemannian volume form. For a compactly supported continuous function \(f\), the product \(f\omega_g\) is a compactly supported continuous \(n\)-form, which the integral of forms already covers, since that construction uses only the continuity of the coefficients. We define the integral of \(f\) over \(M\) to be \[ \int_M f\, dV_g := \int_M f\,\omega_g, \] where the notation \(dV_g\) is the traditional name for the volume form in this role. On a surface it is written \(dA_g\), and on a curve \(ds_g\). If \(M\) is itself compact, taking \(f \equiv 1\) gives the volume of \(M\), \[ \operatorname{Vol}(M) := \int_M \omega_g. \]

One warning about the notation is worth stating once. The symbol \(dV_g\) does not denote the exterior derivative of anything. It is a single indivisible name for the volume form. On a nonempty compact manifold without boundary, of dimension \(n \geq 1\), the volume form is never exact. Its integral \(\operatorname{Vol}(M)\) is positive by part (c) of the properties of the integral, since \(\omega_g\) is a positively oriented orientation form, whereas an exact top form integrates to zero. The \(d\) is purely a typographical inheritance from classical calculus.

Positivity of the Integral

The single analytic fact that distinguishes the integral of a function from that of a form is positivity. A nonnegative function has a nonnegative integral, and the integral vanishes only when the function does. A top-degree form, by contrast, can be called nonnegative only relative to a choice of orientation. It is the metric, through the positivity of \(\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\), that makes this positivity hold for functions.

Proposition: Positivity of the Integral

Let \((M, g)\) be a nonempty oriented Riemannian manifold with or without boundary, and let \(f\) be a compactly supported continuous real-valued function on \(M\) satisfying \(f \geq 0\). Then \[ \int_M f\, dV_g \geq 0, \] with equality if and only if \(f \equiv 0\).

Proof.

If \(f \equiv 0\), then \(f\omega_g = 0\) and the integral is \(0\). Otherwise \(f(p) \gt 0\) at some point \(p\), and we show that the integral is strictly positive, which proves both assertions at once. The form \(f\omega_g\) is everywhere non-negative with respect to the orientation, in the sense of part (c) of the properties of the integral. Indeed, for \(n \geq 1\), at each point \(q\) the volume form takes the value \(1\) on an oriented orthonormal basis of \(T_qM\), and any positively oriented basis differs from that one by a matrix of positive determinant, so \(\omega_g\) is positive on every positively oriented basis, while \(f(q) \geq 0\). For \(n = 0\) the volume form is the orientation sign, and the same conclusion holds in the zero-dimensional reading of part (c). At \(p\) the form \(f\omega_g\) is strictly positive, so part (c) gives \(\int_M f\, dV_g \gt 0\).

For \(n \geq 1\), in coordinates this is the positivity of \(\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\). In a positively oriented chart the volume form is \(\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\,dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n\), so a function supported in the domain of such a chart \((U, \varphi)\) integrates to \(\int_{\varphi(U)} f\,\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\, dx^1 \cdots dx^n\), the integral of a nonnegative function, the factor \(\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\) being strictly positive because \(g\) is positive definite. In a negatively oriented chart the coordinate expression acquires a minus sign, which the definition of the integral over such a chart cancels.

Two consequences are routine but constantly used. Applied to the nonnegative functions \(|f| - f\) and \(|f| + f\), positivity and linearity give \(-\int_M |f|\, dV_g \leq \int_M f\, dV_g \leq \int_M |f|\, dV_g\), the integral triangle inequality \(\left| \int_M f\, dV_g \right| \leq \int_M |f|\, dV_g\). Applied to a difference, it shows that integration is monotone. These are the properties that let the Riemannian integral behave like the integral of elementary calculus, and they are what make the volume form the right measure for analysis on a curved space. The divergence theorem, taken up next, lives in exactly that setting.

The Divergence Theorem

The classical divergence theorem of vector calculus equates the integral of the divergence of a vector field over a region with the flux of that field through the boundary. With the Riemannian integral in hand, the same statement holds for compactly supported vector fields on any positive-dimensional oriented Riemannian manifold with boundary, and its proof is a single application of Stokes's theorem once the right differential-form translations are in place. The work is entirely in setting up two pieces of geometry: a coordinate-free definition of divergence, and a clean identification of the boundary integrand.

The Divergence Operator

The volume form provides a coordinate-free way to measure how a vector field distorts volume. Contracting it against a vector field \(X\) by interior multiplication produces an \((n-1)\)-form \(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g\), and its exterior derivative is again a top-degree form, hence a function multiple of \(\omega_g\). That multiple is the divergence.

Definition: The Divergence of a Vector Field

Let \((M, g)\) be an oriented Riemannian \(n\)-manifold, \(n \geq 1\), with or without boundary, with volume form \(\omega_g\). The divergence of a smooth vector field \(X\) is the unique smooth function \(\operatorname{div} X\) satisfying \[ d\bigl(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g\bigr) = (\operatorname{div} X)\, \omega_g. \] Because \(\omega_g\) is nowhere zero, this equation determines \(\operatorname{div} X\) uniquely at each point.

The resulting function depends neither on coordinates nor on the choice of orientation. Reversing the orientation flips the sign of \(\omega_g\) on both sides, leaving \(\operatorname{div} X\) unchanged. On a nonorientable manifold one applies the definition on each orientable open subset, such as a chart domain, with any orientation of it. Since \(d\) and interior multiplication are local, and the sign argument applies on each component of an overlap, the resulting functions agree on overlaps. So the divergence is well defined on any Riemannian manifold, orientable or not. When \(M\) has no boundary, so that the flow of \(X\) and the Lie derivative along it are available, its meaning is made transparent by Cartan's formula. Since \(\omega_g\) is a top-degree form, \(d\omega_g = 0\), and the Cartan formula \(\mathcal{L}_X \omega_g = X \lrcorner\, d\omega_g + d(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g)\) collapses to \[ \mathcal{L}_X \omega_g = d(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g) = (\operatorname{div} X)\, \omega_g. \] The divergence is therefore exactly the rate at which the flow of \(X\) stretches or compresses the volume form. It is the proportionality factor in the Lie derivative of \(\omega_g\) along \(X\).

In standard coordinates on Euclidean space, where \(\omega_g = dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n\), expanding \(d(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g)\) reproduces the familiar formula \(\operatorname{div} X = \sum_i \partial X^i / \partial x^i\), so the definition extends the classical one.

The Boundary Integrand

To turn Stokes's theorem into a flux statement, the restriction of \(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g\) to the boundary must be identified. The answer is geometrically exactly what one would hope. Only the component of \(X\) normal to the boundary contributes, weighted by the boundary's own volume form.

Lemma: Restriction of the Contracted Volume Form

Let \((M, g)\) be an oriented Riemannian manifold with boundary, let \(N\) be the outward unit normal along \(\partial M\), and let \(\widetilde g\) be the induced metric on \(\partial M\). For any smooth vector field \(X\) on \(M\), \[ \iota_{\partial M}^{*}\bigl(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g\bigr) = \langle X, N \rangle_g\, \omega_{\widetilde g}, \] where \(\omega_{\widetilde g}\) is the volume form of \(\partial M\) with the Stokes orientation.

Proof.

Along \(\partial M\), decompose \(X\) into its normal and tangential parts. Writing \(X^{\perp} = \langle X, N \rangle_g\, N\) for the normal component and \(X^{\top} = X - X^{\perp}\) for the tangential one, the contraction splits by linearity: \[ X \lrcorner\, \omega_g = X^{\perp} \lrcorner\, \omega_g + X^{\top} \lrcorner\, \omega_g. \] For the normal part, \(X^{\perp} = \langle X, N \rangle_g\, N\), so pulling back to the boundary and using that the boundary volume form is \(\iota_{\partial M}^{*}(N \lrcorner\, \omega_g)\) gives \[ \iota_{\partial M}^{*}\bigl(X^{\perp} \lrcorner\, \omega_g\bigr) = \langle X, N \rangle_g\, \iota_{\partial M}^{*}\bigl(N \lrcorner\, \omega_g\bigr) = \langle X, N \rangle_g\, \omega_{\widetilde g}. \] For the tangential part, it suffices to show the pullback of \(X^{\top} \lrcorner\, \omega_g\) vanishes. Evaluated on any \(n-1\) vectors tangent to \(\partial M\), the form \((X^{\top} \lrcorner\, \omega_g)\) feeds \(\omega_g\) the \(n\) vectors \(X^{\top}, V_1, \dots, V_{n-1}\), all of which are tangent to \(\partial M\). But \(\partial M\) is \((n-1)\)-dimensional, so these \(n\) tangent vectors are linearly dependent, and \(\omega_g\), being alternating, vanishes on any linearly dependent set. Hence the tangential term contributes nothing, and the displayed identity follows.

The Theorem

With both pieces in place, the divergence theorem is immediate.

Theorem: The Divergence Theorem

Let \((M, g)\) be a positive-dimensional oriented Riemannian manifold with boundary. For any compactly supported smooth vector field \(X\) on \(M\), \[ \int_M (\operatorname{div} X)\, dV_g = \int_{\partial M} \langle X, N \rangle_g\, dV_{\widetilde g}, \] where \(N\) is the outward unit normal along \(\partial M\) and \(\widetilde g\) is the induced metric on the boundary. In dimension three over a compact region of space, this is the classical theorem of Gauss.

Proof.

By the definition of divergence, the interior integrand is \((\operatorname{div} X)\, \omega_g = d(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g)\). Applying Stokes's theorem to the \((n-1)\)-form \(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g\), we obtain \[ \int_M (\operatorname{div} X)\, dV_g = \int_M d\bigl(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g\bigr) = \int_{\partial M} \iota_{\partial M}^{*}\bigl(X \lrcorner\, \omega_g\bigr). \] The preceding lemma identifies the boundary integrand as \(\langle X, N \rangle_g\, \omega_{\widetilde g}\), which is exactly \(\langle X, N \rangle_g\, dV_{\widetilde g}\). Substituting completes the proof.

The Volume Interpretation Made Precise

The reading of \(\operatorname{div} X\) as the rate of volume change under the flow can be sharpened into an exact dictionary between the sign of the divergence and the volume behavior of the flow. We first say what the volume behavior of a flow means.

Definition: Volume-Preserving and Volume-Increasing Flows

Let \((M, g)\) be an oriented Riemannian \(n\)-manifold without boundary, \(n \geq 1\), and let \(\theta\) be the flow of a smooth vector field on \(M\), with \(M_t\) the domain of \(\theta_t\). For a compact regular domain \(D \subseteq M\), let \(J_D\) be the set of \(t\) with \(D \subseteq M_t\). The flow is volume-preserving if \(\operatorname{Vol}(\theta_t(D)) = \operatorname{Vol}(D)\) for every compact regular domain \(D\) and every \(t \in J_D\). It is volume-nondecreasing, respectively volume-nonincreasing, if for every compact regular domain \(D\) the function \(t \mapsto \operatorname{Vol}(\theta_t(D))\) is nondecreasing, respectively nonincreasing, on \(J_D\). It is strictly volume-increasing, respectively strictly volume-decreasing, if this function is strictly increasing, respectively strictly decreasing, on \(J_D\) for every nonempty compact regular domain \(D\).

Two remarks make the definition meaningful. First, \(J_D\) is an open interval containing \(0\). It is an interval because the maximal integral curve through each point is defined on an open interval containing \(0\), so if \(D \subseteq M_t\) then \(D \subseteq M_s\) for every \(s\) between \(0\) and \(t\). It is open because for \(t_0 \in J_D\) the compact set \(\{t_0\} \times D\) lies in the open flow domain, so some product \((t_0 - \varepsilon, t_0 + \varepsilon) \times D\) does too. Second, for \(t \in J_D\) the map \(\theta_t\) is a diffeomorphism from the open set \(M_t\) onto the open set \(M_{-t}\), so it carries \(D\) onto a compact embedded codimension-\(0\) submanifold with boundary \(\theta_t(D)\). Being compact, this is a closed subset of \(M\), hence properly embedded, that is, a compact regular domain. Its volume \(\int_{\theta_t(D)} \omega_g\), taken with the orientation and volume form restricted from \(M\), is defined. The empty set is excluded from the strict notions only because its volume is identically \(0\).

Proposition: Geometric Interpretation of the Divergence

Let \((M, g)\) be an oriented Riemannian \(n\)-manifold without boundary, \(n \geq 1\), let \(X\) be a smooth vector field, and let \(\theta\) be its flow. Then, in the sense of the definition above, the flow is volume-preserving if and only if \(\operatorname{div} X = 0\) everywhere, volume-nondecreasing if and only if \(\operatorname{div} X \geq 0\) everywhere, and strictly volume-increasing if and only if \(\operatorname{div} X \gt 0\) on a dense subset. The analogous statements hold for the reverse inequalities.

Proof.

Let \(D \subseteq M\) be a compact regular domain. For \(t\) in the open interval \(J_D\) of the definition, \(\theta_t\) is defined on all of \(D\), and the flow carries \(D\) diffeomorphically onto \(\theta_t(D)\). The change-of-variables formula for the volume form, part (d) of the properties of the integral, lets the volume of the image be written as an integral over \(D\) of the pulled-back form \(\theta_t^{*}\omega_g\) (the flow maps \(\theta_t\) are orientation-preserving, because on \(D\) we have \(\theta_t^{*}\omega_g = f_t\,\omega_g\) for a nowhere-vanishing function \(f_t\) that depends continuously on \(t\) and equals \(1\) at \(t = 0\), so \(f_t \gt 0\) on the interval \(J_D\)). We now differentiate under the integral sign at \(t = t_0\). This is legitimate: computing \(\int_D \theta_t^{*}\omega_g\) with a fixed finite family of charts of \(D\) and a subordinate partition of unity, each term is the integral over a fixed compact set of a coefficient that, together with its \(t\)-derivative, is continuous in \((t, x)\) for \(t\) near \(t_0\), because the flow is smooth in \((t, p)\). For such integrands the derivative of the integral is the integral of the derivative, a standard fact of multivariable calculus that we take as background. The \(t\)-derivative of a pullback along a flow is the pullback of the Lie derivative, \(\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t = t_0} \theta_t^{*}\omega_g = \theta_{t_0}^{*}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_X \omega_g\bigr)\), the identity displayed in the proof of the characterization of invariance by a vanishing Lie derivative. Together these give \[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=t_0} \operatorname{Vol}(\theta_t(D)) = \int_D \theta_{t_0}^{*}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_X \omega_g\bigr) = \int_{\theta_{t_0}(D)} (\operatorname{div} X)\, dV_g, \] the last step substituting \(\mathcal{L}_X \omega_g = (\operatorname{div} X)\, \omega_g\) and changing variables back.

If \(\operatorname{div} X = 0\), this derivative vanishes for every \(D\) and every \(t_0\), so the volume is constant and the flow is volume-preserving. The inequality versions follow the same way. For the strict and converse directions, positivity of the integral of a function does the work. Suppose \(\operatorname{div} X \gt 0\) on a dense set. By continuity \(\operatorname{div} X \geq 0\) everywhere, so the derivative is nonnegative. The manifold interior of a regular domain is its topological interior, so for nonempty \(D\) the set \(\operatorname{Int} D\) is an open subset of \(M\), nonempty because a nonempty manifold with boundary has interior points, and its image under the diffeomorphism \(\theta_{t_0}\) is open as well. By density, \(\operatorname{div} X\) is positive at some point of \(\theta_{t_0}(\operatorname{Int} D)\). The positivity of the integral, applied on the compact manifold with boundary \(\theta_{t_0}(D)\), therefore makes \(\int_{\theta_{t_0}(D)} (\operatorname{div} X)\, dV_g\) strictly positive. Hence the derivative is positive at every \(t_0 \in J_D\), and the volume is strictly increasing.

The converses are contrapositives. If \(\operatorname{div} X\) is nonzero at a point, continuity makes it of one sign on a neighborhood. That neighborhood contains a compact regular domain \(D\) with nonempty interior. The derivative of its volume at \(t = 0\) is \(\int_D (\operatorname{div} X)\, dV_g\), which is nonzero with the sign of \(\operatorname{div} X\) there, by the positivity of the integral. So the volume of \(D\) changes, and the flow is not volume-preserving. In the negative case \(\operatorname{Vol}(\theta_t(D)) \lt \operatorname{Vol}(D)\) for small \(t \gt 0\), so the flow is not volume-nondecreasing either. If instead \(\operatorname{div} X\) fails to be positive on a dense set, some nonempty open set \(W\) carries \(\operatorname{div} X \leq 0\). Choose a compact regular domain \(D \subseteq W\) with nonempty interior. The set of \((t, p)\) in the flow domain with \(\theta(t, p) \in W\) is open and contains \(\{0\} \times D\), so by compactness of \(D\) there is \(\delta \gt 0\) with \((-\delta, \delta) \times D\) inside that set, which places \((-\delta, \delta)\) inside \(J_D\) and gives \(\theta_t(D) \subseteq W\) for \(|t| \lt \delta\). For such \(t\) the derivative \(\int_{\theta_t(D)} (\operatorname{div} X)\, dV_g\) is \(\leq 0\), so the volume of \(D\) is nonincreasing on \((-\delta, \delta)\), and the flow is not strictly volume-increasing. The derivative at \(t = 0\) alone would not suffice here, since a strictly increasing function can have zero derivative at a point.

Surface Integrals and the Classical Stokes Theorem

The theorem that originally bore the name of Stokes concerned the circulation of a vector field around the boundary of a surface in space, equated with the flux of its curl through the surface. That classical result is the three-dimensional shadow of the general theorem, recovered once the curl is given an intrinsic definition. Unlike divergence, which makes sense in every dimension, the curl is special to dimension three, and seeing why is half the content of this section.

Gradient and Curl from the Metric

On a Riemannian manifold the metric converts between vector fields and \(1\)-forms through the musical isomorphisms. Lowering an index sends a vector field \(X\) to the \(1\)-form \(X^\flat = \langle X, \cdot \rangle_g\), and raising an index sends a \(1\)-form back to a vector field. The gradient of a function is defined by raising the index on its differential, \[ \operatorname{grad} f = (df)^\sharp, \] so that \(\langle \operatorname{grad} f, Y \rangle_g = df(Y) = Yf\) for every \(Y\). This is the coordinate-free version of the classical gradient, and it works in every dimension.

The curl is more delicate. Given a vector field \(X\), the \(1\)-form \(X^\flat\) has an exterior derivative \(d(X^\flat)\), which is a \(2\)-form. To turn a \(2\)-form back into a vector field we would need a correspondence between \(2\)-forms and vector fields, and on an \(n\)-manifold the space of \(2\)-forms has dimension \(\binom{n}{2}\) while vector fields correspond to \(1\)-forms, of dimension \(n\). For \(n \geq 1\) these match, \(\binom{n}{2} = n\), only when \(n = 3\). In that dimension alone, contraction with the volume form gives a bijection between vector fields and \(2\)-forms, and the curl is defined by inverting it.

Definition: The Curl of a Vector Field

Let \((M, g)\) be an oriented Riemannian \(3\)-manifold, with or without boundary. The curl of a smooth vector field \(X\) is the unique smooth vector field \(\operatorname{curl} X\) satisfying \[ (\operatorname{curl} X) \lrcorner\, \omega_g = d\bigl(X^\flat\bigr). \] The contraction \(Y \mapsto Y \lrcorner\, \omega_g\) is a bijection from vector fields to \(2\)-forms in dimension three, so \(\operatorname{curl} X\) is well defined. At each point it is a linear map from \(T_pM\) to the alternating \(2\)-tensors on \(T_pM\). It is injective: if \(Y \neq 0\) is extended to a basis \((Y, v_2, v_3)\), then \((Y \lrcorner\, \omega_g)(v_2, v_3) = \omega_g(Y, v_2, v_3) \neq 0\). Both spaces have dimension three, so it is an isomorphism. The pointwise inverse is smooth: in a local oriented orthonormal frame \((E_i)\), obtained by Gram–Schmidt from a positively oriented local frame, with dual coframe \((\varepsilon^i)\), the contraction sends \(E_1, E_2, E_3\) to \(\varepsilon^2 \wedge \varepsilon^3\), \(\varepsilon^3 \wedge \varepsilon^1\), \(\varepsilon^1 \wedge \varepsilon^2\), so a smooth \(2\)-form, having smooth coefficients in this basis, is the image of a smooth vector field.

With gradient, curl, and divergence all expressed through the exterior derivative, the three classical operators are revealed as a single operator \(d\) read in three successive degrees. Gradient is \(d\) on functions, curl is \(d\) on \(1\)-forms, and divergence is \(d\) on \((n-1)\)-forms, all transported across the musical isomorphisms and the volume-form contraction. This is the intrinsic form of the grad-curl-div correspondence that appears for Euclidean space. The two classical identities of vector calculus, that the curl of a gradient and the divergence of a curl both vanish, are once again the single fact \(d \circ d = 0\).

Surface Integrals

Let \(S\) be a compact oriented \(2\)-dimensional embedded submanifold with boundary inside an oriented Riemannian \(3\)-manifold \(M\), with or without boundary, and let \(N\) be a smooth unit normal field along \(S\) determining its orientation. The induced metric gives \(S\) its own area form \(dA\), and the surface integral of a vector field \(X\) over \(S\) is the integral of the normal component, \[ \int_S \langle X, N \rangle_g\, dA, \] the flux of \(X\) through \(S\).

The volume form of a hypersurface gives \(dA = \iota_S^{*}(N \lrcorner\, \omega_g)\), so this flux integral is itself the integral over \(S\) of the \(2\)-form \(N \lrcorner\, \omega_g\) weighted by \(\langle X, N \rangle_g\). That weighting is the surface-integral analogue of the boundary integrand from the divergence theorem. The proof of the divergence lemma used only that \(N\) is a unit normal along a hypersurface. The same decomposition therefore applies with \(\partial M\) replaced by \(S\), giving \(\iota_S^{*}(Y \lrcorner\, \omega_g) = \langle Y, N \rangle_g\, dA\) for every smooth vector field \(Y\).

The Classical Stokes Theorem

Everything is now in place to recover the surface form of Stokes's theorem. The flux of the curl through a surface equals the circulation of the field around its boundary.

Theorem: The Classical Stokes Theorem

Let \(M\) be an oriented Riemannian \(3\)-manifold, with or without boundary, and let \(S \subseteq M\) be a compact oriented \(2\)-dimensional embedded submanifold with boundary, with unit normal \(N\) determining its orientation. For any smooth vector field \(X\) on \(M\), \[ \int_S \langle \operatorname{curl} X, N \rangle_g\, dA = \int_{\partial S} \langle X, T \rangle_g\, ds, \] where \(\partial S\) carries the Stokes orientation induced from \(S\), \(T\) is the unit tangent field along \(\partial S\) that is positively oriented for it, and \(ds\) is the Riemannian volume form of \(\partial S\) for the induced metric and that orientation, its arc-length form.

Proof.

Apply Stokes's theorem to the \(1\)-form \(X^\flat\) on \(S\): \[ \int_S d\bigl(X^\flat\bigr) = \int_{\partial S} X^\flat. \] The two integrands are exactly the two sides of the claimed identity.

On the left, the surface analogue of the divergence lemma gives \(\iota_S^{*}\,d(X^\flat) = \langle \operatorname{curl} X, N \rangle_g\, dA\). The curl was defined by \((\operatorname{curl} X) \lrcorner\, \omega_g = d(X^\flat)\), and restricting that \(2\)-form to \(S\) extracts the normal component of \(\operatorname{curl} X\) against the area form, exactly as the divergence lemma extracted \(\langle X, N \rangle_g\). On the right, restricting \(X^\flat\) to the curve \(\partial S\) gives \(\langle X, T \rangle_g\, ds\). The \(1\)-form \(\iota_{\partial S}^{*}X^\flat\) on a \(1\)-manifold equals \(f\, ds\) for some function \(f\), and evaluating on the unit tangent \(T\), where \(ds(T) = 1\), gives \(f = X^\flat(T) = \langle X, T \rangle_g\). Substituting both translations yields the stated equality.

This is the theorem of classical vector calculus, now seen as one more reading of \(\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega\). Together with the fundamental theorem for line integrals and the divergence theorem, it completes the recovery of the integral theorems of three-dimensional vector calculus from a single statement about forms. Each is that one equation, specialized to a degree and dressed in the language of the metric. What changes from one to the next is only the dimension and the dictionary entry through which the exterior derivative is read. That entry is the gradient, the curl, or the divergence.
