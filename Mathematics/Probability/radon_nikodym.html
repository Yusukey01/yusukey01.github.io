Why Radon-Nikodym?

Four earlier pages have used the name "Radon-Nikodym" without proof. For the pushforward measure \(P_X\) of a random variable, we defined the probability density \(f_X = dP_X/d\lambda\) and granted its existence whenever \(P_X \ll \lambda\). In Dual Spaces & Riesz Representation and \(L^p\) Spaces, the converse half of \((L^p)^* \cong L^q\) was stated with its proof deferred. That half asserts that every continuous functional on \(L^p\) arises from some \(L^q\) function. And in Limit Theorems & Product Measures, we announced that the Radon-Nikodym theorem ultimately defines conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) for every integrable random variable. All of these invocations rest on the single theorem proved here.

The theorem answers a sharp question: given two measures \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) on the same space, when can \(\nu\) be written as an integral against \(\mu\)? That is, when does there exist a measurable \(f\) with \[ \nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu \quad \text{for every measurable } A \, ? \] The answer turns on a single hypothesis, absolute continuity, written \(\nu \ll \mu\). The function \(f\) it produces is the density of \(\nu\) with respect to \(\mu\), denoted \(d\nu/d\mu\).

To formulate the theorem we first develop signed measures and the Hahn and Jordan decompositions. The proof itself is a Hilbert-space argument due to von Neumann, and it puts the functional-analytic machinery of our analysis pages to work in probability. The theorem's applications in machine learning are pervasive. They include Bayesian posteriors, diffusion-model score functions, importance-sampling reweights, and KL divergences in RLHF, and we collect them once the theorem is in hand.

Signed Measures, Hahn & Jordan Decompositions

A measure, as introduced in Measure Theory, assigns a non-negative value to each measurable set and is countably additive on disjoint unions. Many natural constructions, however, produce set functions that take both signs. The simplest example is the following. Given a measure \(\mu\) and an integrable function \(f \in L^1(\mu)\) that takes both positive and negative values, the assignment \[ \nu_f(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu \] is countably additive and finite, but \(\nu_f(A)\) can be negative. Differences of two measures, \(\nu = \mu_1 - \mu_2\) with at least one of them finite, behave the same way. To work with such objects on equal footing with measures, we relax the non-negativity axiom while keeping countable additivity intact.

Definition

Definition: Signed Measure

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) be a measurable space. A signed measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) is a function \(\nu : \mathcal{F} \to [-\infty, \infty]\) satisfying:

  1. \(\nu(\emptyset) = 0\).
  2. \(\nu\) takes at most one of the values \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\) (so the countable sum in (3) is always well-defined, with no \(\infty - \infty\) ambiguity).
  3. Countable additivity. For any sequence \((A_n)_{n \geq 1}\) of pairwise disjoint sets in \(\mathcal{F}\), \[ \nu\!\left(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \nu(A_n), \] where the series \(\sum_n \nu(A_n)\) is required to converge absolutely whenever \(\nu(\bigsqcup_n A_n)\) is finite. (When the left-hand side equals \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\), condition (2) keeps the terms of the opposite sign summable, so the series diverges to that value whatever the ordering.)

The absolute-convergence requirement in (3) deserves a brief comment. When \(\nu(\bigsqcup_n A_n)\) is finite, the left-hand side does not depend on the order in which the disjoint sets \(A_n\) are listed, so the series on the right must converge to the same value under every rearrangement. By Riemann's rearrangement theorem on real series, this is equivalent to absolute convergence. The condition is automatic when \(\nu\) is non-negative. Only the possibility of cancellation between positive and negative terms makes it substantive in the signed case.

The example \(\nu_f(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu\) is the prototype. Splitting \(f = f^+ - f^-\) into its positive and negative parts gives \[ \begin{align*} \nu_f(A) &= \int_A f^+ \, d\mu - \int_A f^- \, d\mu \\\\ &= \nu_{f^+}(A) - \nu_{f^-}(A), \end{align*} \] which writes \(\nu_f\) as the difference of two non-negative measures, supported respectively on \(\{f \geq 0\}\) and \(\{f \lt 0\}\). The Jordan decomposition asserts that every signed measure admits such a structural splitting canonically, independently of any representing function \(f\).

The Hahn and Jordan Decompositions

The geometric picture is direct. A signed measure \(\nu\) on \(\Omega\) carves the space into a part \(P\) on which \(\nu\) is non-negative and a complementary part \(N\) on which \(\nu\) is non-positive. Once such a decomposition of \(\Omega\) is found, the splitting of \(\nu\) into a non-negative and a non-positive piece comes for free.

A measurable set \(P \in \mathcal{F}\) is called positive for \(\nu\) if \(\nu(A) \geq 0\) for every measurable \(A \subseteq P\). Similarly, \(N\) is negative if \(\nu(A) \leq 0\) for every measurable \(A \subseteq N\). It is not enough merely that \(\nu(P) \geq 0\). Every measurable subset of \(P\) must inherit non-negativity.

Theorem: Hahn Decomposition

Let \(\nu\) be a signed measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\). Then there exist disjoint measurable sets \(P, N \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(\Omega = P \sqcup N\), where \(P\) is positive and \(N\) is negative for \(\nu\). The decomposition is unique up to \(\nu\)-null sets. If \(\Omega = P' \sqcup N'\) is another such decomposition, then the symmetric differences \(P \triangle P'\) and \(N \triangle N'\) are \(\nu\)-null in the strong sense (every measurable subset has \(\nu\)-measure zero).

Proof:

We assume without loss of generality that \(\nu\) does not take the value \(+\infty\). The other case is symmetric. The strategy is to extract a positive set of maximal measure and verify that its complement is negative.

Step 1 (subset extraction). We first show that if \(E \in \mathcal{F}\) satisfies \(0 \lt \nu(E) \lt \infty\), then \(E\) contains a positive set \(A \subseteq E\) with \(\nu(A) \gt 0\).

Suppose for contradiction that every measurable \(A \subseteq E\) with \(\nu(A) \gt 0\) fails to be positive, that is, contains a measurable subset of strictly negative \(\nu\)-value. Set \(E_0 = E\). Let \(n_1\) be the smallest positive integer such that there exists a measurable \(B_1 \subseteq E_0\) with \(\nu(B_1) \lt -1/n_1\). Such an \(n_1\) exists by the contradiction hypothesis. Set \(E_1 = E_0 \setminus B_1\). Inductively, given \(E_{k-1}\), let \(n_k\) be the smallest positive integer such that there exists \(B_k \subseteq E_{k-1}\) with \(\nu(B_k) \lt -1/n_k\), and set \(E_k = E_{k-1} \setminus B_k\). Continue while such an \(n_k\) exists.

If at some stage no such \(n_k\) exists, then no measurable subset of \(E_{k-1}\) has strictly negative \(\nu\)-measure, so \(E_{k-1}\) is itself a positive set. Moreover, \[ \begin{align*} \nu(E_{k-1}) &= \nu(E) - \sum_{j \lt k} \nu(B_j) \\\\ &= \nu(E) + \sum_{j \lt k} |\nu(B_j)| \\\\ &\geq \nu(E) \\\\ &\gt 0 \end{align*} \] (each \(\nu(B_j)\) is negative, and it is finite because \(\nu(E) = \nu(B_j) + \nu(E \setminus B_j)\) is finite, so \(-\nu(B_j) = |\nu(B_j)| \geq 0\)), and the claim holds with \(A = E_{k-1}\). Otherwise, the construction continues for all \(k \geq 1\), and we proceed as follows.

Define \(E_\infty = E \setminus \bigsqcup_{k \geq 1} B_k\), so that \(E = E_\infty \sqcup \bigsqcup_{k \geq 1} B_k\). By countable additivity, \[ \nu(E) = \nu(E_\infty) + \sum_{k \geq 1} \nu(B_k). \] Since \(\nu(E)\) is finite and \(\nu\) does not take the value \(+\infty\) (by the WLOG assumption), the equation forces both \(\nu(E_\infty)\) and \(\sum_k \nu(B_k)\) to be finite. Indeed, \(\nu(E_\infty) \lt +\infty\) by the same assumption, the series has only negative terms, and if either summand were \(-\infty\), the right-hand side would be \(-\infty \neq \nu(E)\). With the left-hand side finite, condition (3) of the signed-measure definition gives absolute convergence of \(\sum_k \nu(B_k)\). Each \(\nu(B_k) \lt -1/n_k \lt 0\), so \(\sum_k 1/n_k \lt \infty\), forcing \(n_k \to \infty\). Moreover, \(\nu(E_\infty) = \nu(E) - \sum_k \nu(B_k) \geq \nu(E) \gt 0\), so in particular \(\nu(E_\infty) \gt 0\).

We claim \(E_\infty\) is positive. If not, there exists \(C \subseteq E_\infty\) with \(\nu(C) \lt 0\). Choose \(m \in \mathbb{N}\) with \(-1/m \gt \nu(C)\) (possible since \(\nu(C) \lt 0\)). For all sufficiently large \(k\), \(n_k \gt m\), so by minimality of \(n_k\), no measurable subset of \(E_{k-1}\) has \(\nu\)-measure \(\lt -1/m\). But \(C \subseteq E_\infty \subseteq E_{k-1}\) and \(\nu(C) \lt -1/m\), a contradiction. Hence \(E_\infty\) is positive with \(\nu(E_\infty) \gt 0\), proving the claim with \(A := E_\infty\).

Step 2 (maximization). Let \[ s = \sup\bigl\{\, \nu(P) \,:\, P \in \mathcal{F} \text{ is positive for } \nu \,\bigr\} \in [0, +\infty]. \] The supremum is over a non-empty family (the empty set is positive with measure \(0\)). The finiteness \(s \lt \infty\) is not yet established. It will follow at the end of this step from \(\nu(P) = s\) and the WLOG assumption \(\nu \lt +\infty\). Choose positive sets \(P_n\) with \(\nu(P_n) \to s\), and set \(P = \bigcup_n P_n\).

Each finite union \(P_1 \cup \cdots \cup P_n\) is positive, since a measurable subset of a finite union of positive sets can be partitioned into measurable pieces, each contained in some \(P_i\), and a sum of non-negative numbers is non-negative. We use continuity from below along the increasing sequence of positive sets \(Q_n := P_1 \cup \cdots \cup P_n \nearrow P\). This is a special case of countable additivity applied to the disjoint sequence \(P_1, P_2 \setminus P_1, P_3 \setminus (P_1 \cup P_2), \ldots\). The values \(\nu(Q_n)\) lie in \([0, s]\), so all quantities are non-negative reals or \(+\infty\) and no \(\infty - \infty\) ambiguity arises. Every measurable subset \(A \subseteq P\) therefore satisfies \(\nu(A) = \lim_n \nu(A \cap Q_n) \geq 0\) (each term is the \(\nu\)-measure of a measurable subset of the positive set \(Q_n\)), so \(P\) is positive.

Moreover, since \(P\) is positive and \(P_n \subseteq P\), the set \(P \setminus P_n\) is a measurable subset of \(P\), hence has non-negative \(\nu\)-measure, giving \(\nu(P) \geq \nu(P_n)\) for each \(n\). Letting \(n \to \infty\) yields \(\nu(P) \geq s\), and since \(\nu(P)\) is a candidate in the supremum, \(\nu(P) = s\). Finally, the WLOG assumption \(\nu \lt +\infty\) gives \(\nu(P) \lt +\infty\), confirming \(s \lt \infty\).

Step 3 (complement is negative). Set \(N = \Omega \setminus P\). Suppose for contradiction that \(N\) is not negative, so that some \(E \subseteq N\) has \(\nu(E) \gt 0\). Since \(\nu(E)\) is finite (as \(\nu \lt +\infty\)), Step 1 produces a positive set \(A \subseteq E\) with \(\nu(A) \gt 0\). Then \(P \cup A\) is positive, disjointly assembled, with \(\nu(P \cup A) = \nu(P) + \nu(A) = s + \nu(A) \gt s\), contradicting the definition of \(s\). Hence \(N\) is negative.

Uniqueness. Let \(\Omega = P' \sqcup N'\) be another Hahn decomposition. The set \(P \setminus P' = P \cap N'\) is a subset of the positive set \(P\) and of the negative set \(N'\), so every measurable \(B \subseteq P \setminus P'\) satisfies both \(\nu(B) \geq 0\) and \(\nu(B) \leq 0\), forcing \(\nu(B) = 0\). Thus \(P \setminus P'\) is \(\nu\)-null in the strong sense. Symmetrically, \(P' \setminus P\) is \(\nu\)-null. Hence \(P \triangle P'\) is \(\nu\)-null, and likewise \(N \triangle N'\).

The Hahn decomposition produces a partition of the underlying space. The Jordan decomposition repackages this partition as an intrinsic splitting of the measure itself.

Theorem: Jordan Decomposition

Every signed measure \(\nu\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) decomposes uniquely as \[ \nu = \nu^+ - \nu^-, \] where \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\) are non-negative measures and \(\nu^+ \perp \nu^-\), that is, \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\) are concentrated on disjoint measurable sets.

Proof:

Let \(\Omega = P \sqcup N\) be a Hahn decomposition for \(\nu\). Define \[ \nu^+(A) = \nu(A \cap P), \quad \nu^-(A) = -\,\nu(A \cap N), \quad A \in \mathcal{F}. \] Since \(P\) is positive and \(N\) is negative, both \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\) are non-negative, and countable additivity of \(\nu\) transfers immediately. For every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), \[ \nu^+(A) - \nu^-(A) = \nu(A \cap P) + \nu(A \cap N) = \nu(A), \] proving the existence of the decomposition. By construction \(\nu^+(N) = 0\) and \(\nu^-(P) = 0\), so \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\) are concentrated on disjoint sets and hence mutually singular.

For uniqueness, suppose \(\nu = \mu_1 - \mu_2\) with \(\mu_1, \mu_2\) non-negative measures concentrated on disjoint measurable sets \(P', N' \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(\Omega = P' \sqcup N'\) (so \(\mu_1(N') = 0\) and \(\mu_2(P') = 0\)).

Step (a): \(\Omega = P' \sqcup N'\) is a Hahn decomposition for \(\nu\). For \(A \subseteq P'\), \(\mu_2(A) \leq \mu_2(P') = 0\) (since \(\mu_2\) is concentrated on \(N'\), disjoint from \(P'\)), so \(\nu(A) = \mu_1(A) \geq 0\). Thus \(P'\) is positive. Symmetrically, \(N'\) is negative. By Hahn uniqueness, the symmetric difference \(P \triangle P' = (P \setminus P') \sqcup (P' \setminus P)\) is \(\nu\)-null in the strong sense.

Step (b): \(\mu_1(P \triangle P') = 0\) and \(\mu_2(P \triangle P') = 0\). For \(P' \setminus P \subseteq P'\), the concentration of \(\mu_2\) on \(N'\) gives \(\mu_2(P' \setminus P) = 0\), so \(\nu(P' \setminus P) = \mu_1(P' \setminus P)\). Together with the strong \(\nu\)-nullity from Step (a), this gives \(\mu_1(P' \setminus P) = 0\). For \(P \setminus P' \subseteq N'\) (since \(P \setminus P' \subseteq \Omega \setminus P' = N'\)), the concentration of \(\mu_1\) on \(P'\) gives \(\mu_1(P \setminus P') = 0\) directly. Then \(\nu(P \setminus P') = -\mu_2(P \setminus P')\), and the strong \(\nu\)-nullity forces \(\mu_2(P \setminus P') = 0\). Combining, \(\mu_1(P \triangle P') = 0\) and \(\mu_2(P \triangle P') = 0\).

Step (c): \(\mu_1 = \nu^+\) and \(\mu_2 = \nu^-\). Fix \(A \in \mathcal{F}\). Since \(\mu_1\) is concentrated on \(P' = (P \cap P') \sqcup (P' \setminus P)\), \[ \begin{align*} \mu_1(A) &= \mu_1(A \cap P') \\\\ &= \mu_1(A \cap P \cap P') + \mu_1(A \cap (P' \setminus P)) \\\\ &= \mu_1(A \cap P \cap P'), \end{align*} \] where the last equality uses \(\mu_1(P' \setminus P) = 0\) from Step (b). Similarly, splitting \(P = (P \cap P') \sqcup (P \setminus P')\), \[ \begin{align*} \mu_1(A \cap P) &= \mu_1(A \cap P \cap P') + \mu_1(A \cap (P \setminus P')) \\\\ &= \mu_1(A \cap P \cap P'), \end{align*} \] using \(\mu_1(P \setminus P') = 0\). Hence \(\mu_1(A) = \mu_1(A \cap P)\).

On the other hand, from \(\nu = \mu_1 - \mu_2\), \[ \nu^+(A) = \nu(A \cap P) = \mu_1(A \cap P) - \mu_2(A \cap P). \] We claim that \(\mu_2(A \cap P) = 0\). Split \(A \cap P = (A \cap P \cap P') \sqcup (A \cap (P \setminus P'))\). The first piece satisfies \(\mu_2(A \cap P \cap P') \leq \mu_2(P') = 0\), and the second satisfies \(\mu_2(A \cap (P \setminus P')) \leq \mu_2(P \setminus P') = 0\) by Step (b). Thus \(\nu^+(A) = \mu_1(A \cap P) = \mu_1(A)\). The identity \(\mu_2 = \nu^-\) follows symmetrically.

The Hahn decomposition is unique only up to \(\nu\)-null sets, but the Jordan decomposition itself is fully unique. The ambiguity in choosing \(P\) versus \(P'\) is invisible to \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\), which depend only on how \(\nu\) acts on sets and not on which Hahn partition was used to construct them.

Definition: Total Variation

The total variation of a signed measure \(\nu\) is the non-negative measure \[ |\nu| = \nu^+ + \nu^-. \] We say \(\nu\) is a finite signed measure if \(|\nu|(\Omega) \lt \infty\), and \(\sigma\)-finite if \(\Omega\) is a countable union of sets of finite \(|\nu|\)-measure.

The total variation \(|\nu|\) is the natural "size" of a signed measure. The value \(|\nu|(A)\) measures the total mass of \(\nu\) on \(A\) without cancellation between the positive and negative parts. For \(\nu_f(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu\) with \(f \in L^1(\mu)\), the Hahn decomposition is given explicitly by \(P = \{f \geq 0\}\) and \(N = \{f \lt 0\}\), so \(\nu_f^+(A) = \int_A f^+ \, d\mu\) and \(\nu_f^-(A) = \int_A f^- \, d\mu\), giving \(|\nu_f|(A) = \int_A |f| \, d\mu\). In particular \(|\nu_f|(\Omega) = \|f\|_{L^1(\mu)}\). The map \(f \mapsto \nu_f\) is therefore a norm-preserving linear embedding of \(L^1(\mu)\) into the space of finite signed measures absolutely continuous with respect to \(\mu\) (in the sense introduced in the next section). For \(\sigma\)-finite \(\mu\), the Radon-Nikodym theorem, proved later on this page, upgrades this embedding to an isometric isomorphism by establishing surjectivity. Every such finite signed measure is of the form \(\nu_f\) for some \(f \in L^1(\mu)\).

Signed Measures in CS and ML

Signed measures appear wherever a system carries net-flow or signed-mass quantities rather than mass alone.

Optimal transport via Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality. In the Earth Mover's formulation of optimal transport, the difference \(P - Q\) of two probability distributions is a signed measure of total mass zero. The Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality writes the Wasserstein-1 distance as a supremum over Lipschitz functions of integrals against this signed measure: \(W_1(P, Q) = \sup_{f \in \mathrm{Lip}_1} \int f \, d(P - Q)\). The Jordan decomposition \(P - Q = (P - Q)^+ - (P - Q)^-\) identifies the regions of mass surplus (sources) and deficit (sinks) between the two distributions. These are the regions from which mass must flow and to which it must arrive in any optimal transport plan.

Log-likelihood ratios. In hypothesis testing and classification, consider the log-likelihood ratio \(\ell(x) = \log\!\bigl(p_1(x)/p_0(x)\bigr)\). When \(\ell \in L^1(P_0)\), that is, when \(\mathbb{E}_{P_0}[|\log(p_1/p_0)|] \lt \infty\), it defines a finite signed measure \(\nu_\ell(A) = \int_A \ell \, dP_0\). Its Jordan decomposition isolates the regions where the evidence favors hypothesis \(H_1\) over \(H_0\) and vice versa. The total variation \(|\nu_\ell|(\Omega) = \mathbb{E}_{P_0}[|\ell|]\) measures the typical magnitude of the log-evidence under \(H_0\). This quantity is related to, but distinct from, the standard divergence-based measures of test difficulty. Examples are the KL divergence \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_0 \| P_1) = -\mathbb{E}_{P_0}[\ell]\) and the total-variation distance \(\mathrm{TV}(P_0, P_1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|p_0 - p_1\|_{L^1}\) (densities taken against a common dominating measure), which gives the minimum Bayes risk under equal priors in binary hypothesis testing via Le Cam's identity \(\mathcal{R}_{\min} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - \mathrm{TV}(P_0, P_1))\).

Network flows on graphs. Consider a flow defined on the edges of a finite graph with sources and sinks. Its divergence, the net flow at each vertex (incoming minus outgoing), is naturally a signed measure \(\nu\) on the vertex set, with \(\nu(\{v\})\) the net flow at vertex \(v\). The Hahn decomposition partitions the vertices into net-source and net-sink subsets. The same algebraic structure underlies divergence operators on simplicial complexes. This connection resurfaces in the simplicial and homological structures developed later in the curriculum, on the way toward Geometric Deep Learning.

Absolute Continuity & Singularity

With the structural theory of signed measures in hand, we turn to the relation between two measures on the same space. The Radon-Nikodym theorem will assert that one measure can be expressed as an integral against another precisely when the two satisfy the relation defined here: absolute continuity. The opposite extreme, mutual singularity, describes measures that are concentrated on disjoint sets and have nothing to integrate against one another. These relations mark the two extremes of how two measures can interact, and the Lebesgue decomposition (mentioned in the Looking Ahead section) shows that they account for every \(\sigma\)-finite case.

Definitions

Definition: Absolute Continuity

Let \(\mu\) be a non-negative measure and \(\nu\) a signed measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\). We say \(\nu\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\mu\), written \(\nu \ll \mu\), if for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), \[ \mu(A) = 0 \Longrightarrow \nu(A) = 0. \]

The condition \(\nu \ll \mu\) says exactly that \(\mu\)-null sets are also \(\nu\)-null sets, so that \(\nu\) inherits whatever \(\mu\) declares to be negligible. For signed \(\nu\), the definition is equivalent to \(|\nu| \ll \mu\), and also to the conjunction \(\nu^+ \ll \mu\) and \(\nu^- \ll \mu\).

To see (\(\Rightarrow\)), suppose \(\mu(A) = 0\). Then \(\mu(B) = 0\) for every measurable \(B \subseteq A\) (by monotonicity, since \(\mu \geq 0\)). Applied to \(B = A \cap P\) and \(B = A \cap N\) from a Hahn decomposition of \(\nu\), this gives \(\nu^+(A) = \nu(A \cap P) = 0\) and \(\nu^-(A) = -\nu(A \cap N) = 0\), hence \(|\nu|(A) = \nu^+(A) + \nu^-(A) = 0\). For (\(\Leftarrow\)), note that \(\mu(A) = 0 \Rightarrow |\nu|(A) = 0 \Rightarrow |\nu(A)| \leq |\nu|(A) = 0 \Rightarrow \nu(A) = 0\).

The Radon-Nikodym theorem will be stated for non-negative \(\nu\). The case of a \(\sigma\)-finite signed \(\nu\) follows by applying the result to \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\) separately and subtracting.

Definition: Mutual Singularity

Two signed measures \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) are mutually singular, written \(\mu \perp \nu\), if there exists a measurable \(E \in \mathcal{F}\) such that \[ |\mu|(E^c) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad |\nu|(E) = 0. \] Equivalently, \(\mu\) is concentrated on \(E\) and \(\nu\) is concentrated on \(E^c\), so the two measures live on disjoint measurable carriers. (Unlike absolute continuity, mutual singularity is a symmetric relation between two signed measures.)

The two relations are extremes. If \(\nu \ll \mu\), then \(\nu\) is "dominated" by \(\mu\), in the sense that every \(\mu\)-negligible set is also \(\nu\)-negligible. If \(\nu \perp \mu\), then \(\nu\) and \(\mu\) have no overlap whatsoever. The only signed measure \(\nu\) with both \(\nu \ll \mu\) and \(\nu \perp \mu\) is the zero measure. From \(\nu \perp \mu\), choose \(E\) with \(|\nu|(E) = 0\) and \(\mu(E^c) = 0\). Then \(\nu \ll \mu\) forces \(|\nu|(E^c) = 0\), and so \(|\nu|(\Omega) = 0\), giving \(\nu = 0\).

The ε-δ Characterization

The implication \(\mu(A) = 0 \Rightarrow \nu(A) = 0\) in the definition of absolute continuity is a qualitative, "all-or-nothing" condition. It says that \(\nu\) collapses on \(\mu\)-null sets, but it says nothing about how \(\nu\) behaves on sets of small but positive \(\mu\)-measure. For finite measures, however, the condition tightens, and \(\nu(A)\) must in fact be uniformly small whenever \(\mu(A)\) is small. This is the analytic counterpart of the qualitative definition, and it is the form in which absolute continuity most often appears in proofs.

Theorem: ε-δ Characterization of Absolute Continuity

Let \(\mu\) be a non-negative measure and \(\nu\) a finite signed measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\). Then \(\nu \ll \mu\) if and only if for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) such that for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), \[ \mu(A) \lt \delta \Longrightarrow |\nu(A)| \lt \varepsilon. \]

Proof:

(\(\Leftarrow\)) The ε-δ condition implies \(\nu \ll \mu\) directly. If \(\mu(A) = 0\), then \(\mu(A) \lt \delta\) for every \(\delta \gt 0\), so \(|\nu(A)| \lt \varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\), forcing \(\nu(A) = 0\).

(\(\Rightarrow\)) Suppose \(\nu \ll \mu\) but the ε-δ condition fails. Then there exists \(\varepsilon_0 \gt 0\) such that for every \(n \geq 1\), some \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) satisfies \[ \mu(A_n) \lt 2^{-n} \quad \text{and} \quad |\nu(A_n)| \geq \varepsilon_0. \] Set \(B_n = \bigcup_{k \geq n} A_k\) and \(B = \bigcap_{n \geq 1} B_n = \limsup_n A_n\). The sets \(B_n\) are decreasing, with \(B_1\) of finite \(|\nu|\)-measure (since \(\nu\) is finite). Writing \(B_n\) as the disjoint union of \(A_n\) and the sets \(A_k \setminus (A_n \cup \cdots \cup A_{k-1})\), \(k \gt n\), each contained in \(A_k\), countable additivity and monotonicity give \[ \mu(B) \leq \mu(B_n) \leq \sum_{k \geq n} \mu(A_k) \lt \sum_{k \geq n} 2^{-k} = 2^{-(n-1)}, \] so \(\mu(B) = 0\) on letting \(n \to \infty\). By absolute continuity, \(|\nu|(B) = 0\).

On the other hand, by continuity from above for the finite measure \(|\nu|\), \[ \begin{align*} |\nu|(B) &= \lim_{n \to \infty} |\nu|(B_n) \\\\ &\geq \limsup_{n \to \infty} |\nu|(A_n) \\\\ &\geq \limsup_{n \to \infty} |\nu(A_n)| \\\\ &\geq \varepsilon_0, \end{align*} \] where the first inequality uses \(A_n \subseteq B_n\), which gives \(|\nu|(A_n) \leq |\nu|(B_n)\) for each \(n\) and hence \(\limsup_n |\nu|(A_n) \leq \lim_n |\nu|(B_n)\), the limit existing because \((B_n)\) is decreasing. The second inequality uses \(|\nu(A_n)| \leq |\nu|(A_n)\), and the third uses \(|\nu(A_n)| \geq \varepsilon_0\). This contradicts \(|\nu|(B) = 0\).

The finiteness hypothesis on \(\nu\) cannot be dropped. Take \(\mu = \lambda\) (Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}\)) and \(\nu(A) = \int_A x^2 \, d\lambda(x)\), so that \(\nu \ll \lambda\) and \(\nu\) is \(\sigma\)-finite. The sets \(A_n = [n, n + 1/n^2]\) satisfy \(\lambda(A_n) = 1/n^2 \to 0\), but \[ \nu(A_n) = \int_n^{n + 1/n^2} x^2 \, dx \geq n^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n^2} = 1 \] for every \(n\). So \(\nu(A_n)\) does not vanish even as \(\lambda(A_n) \to 0\), and the ε-δ form fails despite \(\nu \ll \lambda\) holding qualitatively.

The Probabilistic Picture

For a probability distribution \(P_X\) on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\), the relation to Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) determines the qualitative type of the random variable. If \(P_X \ll \lambda\), the distribution is absolutely continuous in the measure-theoretic sense. It has a Lebesgue density, namely the Radon-Nikodym derivative \(dP_X/d\lambda\), whose existence we prove below. If \(P_X \perp \lambda\), there is no density relative to length. This happens, for instance, when \(P_X\) is concentrated on a countable set, or on a fractal of zero Lebesgue measure such as the Cantor set.

A mixed distribution, such as \(\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda\big|_{[0,1]}\), is neither. It has a singular part (the Dirac mass at \(0\)) and an absolutely continuous part. The Lebesgue decomposition, mentioned below, asserts that this pattern is universal. Every \(\sigma\)-finite measure splits canonically into an absolutely continuous piece and a mutually singular piece.

The Radon-Nikodym Theorem

We arrive at the central result. Given two \(\sigma\)-finite measures with \(\nu \ll \mu\), the Radon-Nikodym theorem asserts that \(\nu\) is the integral of some non-negative measurable function against \(\mu\). This function, unique up to \(\mu\)-null sets, is the abstract derivative \(d\nu/d\mu\) and the working object underneath every "density" in probability and statistics.

Statement

Theorem: Radon-Nikodym

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) be a measurable space, and let \(\mu, \nu\) be \(\sigma\)-finite non-negative measures on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) with \(\nu \ll \mu\). Then there exists a non-negative measurable function \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\), unique up to \(\mu\)-a.e. equality, such that \[ \nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu \quad \text{for every } A \in \mathcal{F}. \] The function \(f\) is called the Radon-Nikodym derivative of \(\nu\) with respect to \(\mu\), denoted \[ f = \frac{d\nu}{d\mu}. \]

For \(\sigma\)-finite signed \(\nu\), the result extends by Jordan decomposition. Writing \(\nu = \nu^+ - \nu^-\) with \(\nu^\pm \ll \mu\) (which follows from \(\nu \ll \mu\), as observed in the previous section), we obtain \(d\nu/d\mu = d\nu^+/d\mu - d\nu^-/d\mu\), an extended-real measurable function, finite \(\mu\)-a.e. when \(\nu\) is finite. The difference is well-defined \(\mu\)-a.e., because \(\nu^+\) vanishes on the negative set of a Hahn decomposition of \(\nu\), which forces \(d\nu^+/d\mu = 0\) \(\mu\)-a.e. there, and symmetrically \(d\nu^-/d\mu = 0\) \(\mu\)-a.e. on the positive set.

Proof via von Neumann's \(L^2\) Method:

The proof we present is von Neumann's, and it applies the Hilbert-space machinery of our functional-analysis pages. Its key idea is to construct \(f\) as the solution of a single linear-algebraic problem in \(L^2\) and then transport it back to a relation between measures. Integration against \(\nu\) defines a bounded linear functional on a suitable \(L^2\) space, and the Riesz Representation Theorem delivers a representing function. Algebraic manipulation then extracts \(f\) from that representative.

Reduction to finite measures.
Since both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are \(\sigma\)-finite, we can write \(\Omega = \bigsqcup_{n \geq 1} \Omega_n\) with \(\Omega_n\) pairwise disjoint, measurable, and satisfying \(\mu(\Omega_n), \nu(\Omega_n) \lt \infty\). (Take a \(\mu\)-exhaustion and a \(\nu\)-exhaustion separately, intersect, and disjointify.) Suppose we can produce a Radon-Nikodym derivative \(f_n\) of \(\nu|_{\Omega_n}\) with respect to \(\mu|_{\Omega_n}\) for each \(n\). Extending each \(f_n\) by zero outside \(\Omega_n\), we obtain a measurable non-negative function \(f = \sum_n f_n \mathbf{1}_{\Omega_n}\). The disjointness of \(\{\Omega_n\}\) together with the Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT) gives \(\nu(A) = \sum_n \nu(A \cap \Omega_n) = \sum_n \int_{A \cap \Omega_n} f_n \, d\mu = \int_A f \, d\mu\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\). Thus it suffices to prove the theorem when both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite.

Step 1: The auxiliary measure \(\rho = \mu + \nu\).
Assume henceforth that \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite. Define a new measure \[ \rho = \mu + \nu, \quad \rho(A) = \mu(A) + \nu(A). \] Then \(\rho\) is finite, and \(\mu \ll \rho\) and \(\nu \ll \rho\) hold trivially (any \(\rho\)-null set has both \(\mu\)- and \(\nu\)-measure zero, since \(\mu, \nu \geq 0\)).

Step 2: A bounded linear functional on \(L^2(\rho)\).
Define \[ T : L^2(\rho) \to \mathbb{R}, \quad T(g) = \int_\Omega g \, d\nu. \] The functional \(T\) is well-defined on \(\rho\)-a.e. equivalence classes because \(\nu \ll \rho\), and the estimate below shows that every \(g \in L^2(\rho)\) is \(\nu\)-integrable. It is linear by linearity of the integral. To verify boundedness, apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on \(L^2(\rho)\) using the constant function \(\mathbf{1} \in L^2(\rho)\) (which lies in \(L^2(\rho)\) because \(\rho(\Omega) \lt \infty\)): \[ \begin{align*} |T(g)| &\leq \int |g| \, d\nu \\\\ &\leq \int |g| \, d\rho \\\\ &= \int |g| \cdot 1 \, d\rho \\\\ &\leq \|g\|_{L^2(\rho)} \cdot \rho(\Omega)^{1/2}. \end{align*} \] Here the second inequality uses \(\nu \leq \rho\) (as measures), and the final inequality is Cauchy-Schwarz. Thus \(T\) is a continuous linear functional on the Hilbert space \(L^2(\rho)\), with operator norm at most \(\rho(\Omega)^{1/2}\).

Step 3: Riesz representation.
By the Riesz Representation Theorem, there exists a unique \(h \in L^2(\rho)\) such that \[ T(g) = \langle g, h \rangle_{L^2(\rho)} = \int_\Omega g \, h \, d\rho \quad \text{for every } g \in L^2(\rho). \] Unwinding the definition of \(T\), this reads \[ \int_\Omega g \, d\nu = \int_\Omega g \, h \, d\rho \quad \text{for every } g \in L^2(\rho). \tag{$\star$} \] So far \(h\) is only an abstract element of \(L^2(\rho)\). The next two steps pin down its geometry.

Step 4: \(0 \leq h \leq 1\) holds \(\rho\)-a.e.
We prove the two bounds separately by testing (\(\star\)) against indicator functions.

For the lower bound, let \(E_- = \{h \lt 0\}\), and substitute \(g = \mathbf{1}_{E_-} \in L^2(\rho)\) (this is in \(L^2(\rho)\) because \(\rho\) is finite). Then \[ \nu(E_-) = \int_{E_-} 1 \, d\nu = \int_{E_-} h \, d\rho \leq 0, \] while \(\nu(E_-) \geq 0\) since \(\nu\) is non-negative. Hence \(\nu(E_-) = 0\) and \(\int_{E_-} h \, d\rho = 0\). But \(h \lt 0\) strictly on \(E_-\), so \(\int_{E_-} h \, d\rho \lt 0\) unless \(\rho(E_-) = 0\). Combined with \(\int_{E_-} h \, d\rho = 0\), this forces \(\rho(E_-) = 0\), that is, \(h \geq 0\) holds \(\rho\)-a.e.

For the upper bound, let \(E_+ = \{h \gt 1\}\) and substitute \(g = \mathbf{1}_{E_+}\). Then \[ \begin{align*} \nu(E_+) &= \int_{E_+} h \, d\rho \\\\ &\geq \int_{E_+} 1 \, d\rho \\\\ &= \rho(E_+) \\\\ &= \mu(E_+) + \nu(E_+). \end{align*} \] Since \(\nu(E_+) \leq \nu(\Omega) \lt \infty\) (we are in the finite case), the term \(\nu(E_+)\) can be subtracted from both sides, forcing \(\mu(E_+) \leq 0\), hence \(\mu(E_+) = 0\). By absolute continuity \(\nu \ll \mu\), this gives \(\nu(E_+) = 0\), and therefore \(\rho(E_+) = 0\). Hence \(h \leq 1\) holds \(\rho\)-a.e.

We may modify \(h\) on a \(\rho\)-null set without disturbing equation (\(\star\)), so we redefine \(h\) to take values in \([0, 1]\) everywhere.

Step 5: Rewriting (\(\star\)) and isolating \(d\nu/d\mu\).
Substitute the definition \(\rho = \mu + \nu\) into (\(\star\)) and use linearity of the integral with respect to the sum measure (\(\int \phi \, d(\mu + \nu) = \int \phi \, d\mu + \int \phi \, d\nu\) for non-negative or \(\rho\)-integrable \(\phi\)): \[ \int_\Omega g \, d\nu = \int_\Omega g \, h \, d\mu + \int_\Omega g \, h \, d\nu, \] which rearranges to \[ \int_\Omega g \, (1 - h) \, d\nu = \int_\Omega g \, h \, d\mu \quad \text{for every } g \in L^2(\rho). \tag{$\star\star$} \] We will use (\(\star\star\)) on indicator functions to identify \(d\nu/d\mu\).

Let \(E_1 = \{h = 1\}\). Substituting \(g = \mathbf{1}_{E_1}\) in (\(\star\star\)) gives \(0 = \int_{E_1} h \, d\mu = \mu(E_1)\), so \(\mu(E_1) = 0\). By absolute continuity \(\nu \ll \mu\), also \(\nu(E_1) = 0\). Hence \(h \lt 1\) holds \(\mu\)-a.e. and \(\nu\)-a.e.

Set \[ f = \frac{h}{1 - h}, \] a non-negative measurable function with values in \([0, \infty)\), defined \(\mu\)-a.e. We set \(f = 0\) on the \(\mu\)-null set \(E_1\) where \(h = 1\), so that \(f\) is defined everywhere. The choice is irrelevant. The identity \(h = (1-h) f\) holds \(\mu\)-a.e., and will be used in Step 6 to extract \(f\) as the desired Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Step 6: From (\(\star\star\)) to the Radon-Nikodym identity.
We first extend (\(\star\star\)) from \(L^2(\rho)\) to all non-negative measurable \(g\). Both sides of (\(\star\star\)) are integrals of non-negative functions against non-negative measures (since \(0 \leq h \leq 1\) \(\rho\)-a.e., so \(g(1-h) \geq 0\) and \(gh \geq 0\) for \(g \geq 0\)). For indicator functions \(g = \mathbf{1}_A\), (\(\star\star\)) holds since \(\mathbf{1}_A \in L^2(\rho)\). By linearity it holds for non-negative simple \(g\). The standard simple-function approximation \(g_n \uparrow g\), with MCT applied to each side, then extends it to all non-negative measurable \(g\), whether or not \(g \in L^2(\rho)\). Call this extended identity (\(\star\star'\)).

Now apply (\(\star\star'\)) with \(g = \mathbf{1}_A / (1 - h)\). Formally, we use the truncations \(g_N := \mathbf{1}_A \cdot \min(N, 1/(1-h))\), each bounded and measurable (hence in \(L^2(\rho)\) since \(\rho\) is finite, and certainly admissible for the extended (\(\star\star'\))). The function \(1/(1-h)\) is undefined on the set \(E_1 = \{h = 1\}\), but \(E_1\) is both \(\mu\)-null and \(\nu\)-null (Step 5), hence \(\rho\)-null, so the value assigned on \(E_1\) (say 0) is irrelevant for both integrals. Substituting \(g_N\) into (\(\star\star'\)): \[ \int_A \min(N(1-h), 1) \, d\nu = \int_A \min(N, 1/(1-h)) \cdot h \, d\mu. \]

On \(\{h \lt 1\}\), \(\min(N(1-h), 1) \uparrow 1\) and \(\min(N, 1/(1-h)) \cdot h \uparrow h/(1-h) = f\) as \(N \to \infty\). Applying MCT to each side, \[ \nu(A) = \int_A 1 \, d\nu = \int_A f \, d\mu \quad \text{for every } A \in \mathcal{F}, \] completing the proof for finite \(\mu, \nu\). The reduction at the start of the proof extends the result to the \(\sigma\)-finite case.

What Hilbert Space Bought Us

The proof never constructs \(f\) directly. The function \(f = h / (1 - h)\) appears only in the last step. Until then, all the work happens with the auxiliary function \(h \in L^2(\rho)\) produced by Riesz representation. This is the strategic content of von Neumann's argument. A problem about existence of a density is converted into a problem about existence of a representing vector for a continuous linear functional on a Hilbert space, which was already solved in Dual Spaces & Riesz Representation.

The functional-analytic apparatus behind this step is substantial. It includes completeness of \(L^2\) (Riesz-Fischer), orthogonal projection, and the duality \((L^2)^* \cong L^2\). In this single proof, the results of our pages on Hilbert spaces, dual functionals, and \(L^p\) completeness become a statement about probability densities.

Uniqueness

Uniqueness of the Radon-Nikodym derivative is a short, standalone argument that uses only the integrability of \(f\) and the zero-integral lemma.

Suppose \(f_1, f_2\) are two non-negative measurable functions, both satisfying \(\nu(A) = \int_A f_i \, d\mu\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\). We first prove uniqueness when \(\nu\) is finite. The \(\sigma\)-finite case follows by applying the finite argument to each \(\Omega_n\) of a \(\sigma\)-finite exhaustion. Assume henceforth \(\nu(\Omega) \lt \infty\). Then \(\int_\Omega f_i \, d\mu = \nu(\Omega) \lt \infty\), so each \(f_i\) is \(\mu\)-integrable and therefore finite \(\mu\)-a.e. We set \(g = f_1 - f_2\), which is well-defined \(\mu\)-a.e., and give \(g\) the value 0 on the \(\mu\)-null exceptional set where either \(f_i\) is infinite (the choice is irrelevant). With this choice we have \[ \int_A g \, d\mu = \nu(A) - \nu(A) = 0 \quad \text{for every } A \in \mathcal{F}. \]

Apply this to \(A = \{g \gt 0\}\) and to \(A = \{g \lt 0\}\) separately. On the first set, \(g^+ = g\) and \(g^- = 0\), so \(\int g^+ \, d\mu = \int_{\{g \gt 0\}} g \, d\mu = 0\). Since \(g^+\) is non-negative, the Zero Integral Lemma forces \(g^+ = 0\) \(\mu\)-a.e., that is, \(\mu(\{g \gt 0\}) = 0\). The argument on \(\{g \lt 0\}\) is symmetric and gives \(\mu(\{g \lt 0\}) = 0\). Hence \(g = 0\) \(\mu\)-a.e., that is, \(f_1 = f_2\) \(\mu\)-a.e.

The Radon-Nikodym derivative is therefore well-defined as an element of the equivalence class of measurable functions modulo \(\mu\)-null modifications. In particular, equations of the form "\(d\nu/d\mu = f\)" are always understood up to \(\mu\)-a.e. equality.

Chain Rule and Change of Variables

The Radon-Nikodym derivative is genuinely a derivative. It satisfies a chain rule under composition of absolute-continuity relations, and it intertwines with integration in exactly the way the Leibniz notation \(d\nu/d\mu\) suggests. These two properties, the chain rule and the change-of-variables formula, make the derivative usable in computation. Bayesian updates, importance-sampling estimators, and KL divergence computations all rest on the second.

Proposition: Chain Rule and Change of Variables

Let \(\mu, \nu, \lambda\) be \(\sigma\)-finite non-negative measures on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\).

  1. Chain rule. If \(\nu \ll \mu \ll \lambda\), then \(\nu \ll \lambda\) and \[ \frac{d\nu}{d\lambda} = \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} \cdot \frac{d\mu}{d\lambda} \quad \lambda\text{-a.e.} \]
  2. Change of variables. If \(\nu \ll \mu\), then for every measurable \(g : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\), \[ \int_\Omega g \, d\nu = \int_\Omega g \cdot \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} \, d\mu. \] The same identity holds for measurable \(g : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) provided either side is finite when \(g\) is replaced by \(|g|\) (that is, \(g \in L^1(\nu)\) if and only if \(g \cdot d\nu/d\mu \in L^1(\mu)\), and the integrals agree).
Proof:

We prove (2) first, and (1) then follows from it.

Proof of (2).
Set \(f = d\nu/d\mu\), so that \(\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\). We extend this from indicator-set integrals to general non-negative \(g\) by the standard three-step procedure.

Step 1: Indicator functions.
For \(g = \mathbf{1}_A\), the identity \[ \int_\Omega \mathbf{1}_A \, d\nu = \nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu = \int_\Omega \mathbf{1}_A \cdot f \, d\mu \] is the defining property of \(f = d\nu/d\mu\).

Step 2: Non-negative simple functions.
Let \(g = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}\) with \(c_i \geq 0\) and \(A_i \in \mathcal{F}\) pairwise disjoint. By linearity of the integral on both sides and Step 1, \[ \begin{align*} \int_\Omega g \, d\nu &= \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \nu(A_i) \\\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \int_\Omega \mathbf{1}_{A_i} \cdot f \, d\mu \\\\ &= \int_\Omega g \cdot f \, d\mu. \end{align*} \]

Step 3: Non-negative measurable functions.
Let \(g : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) be measurable. By the standard simple-function approximation, there exists an increasing sequence \(g_n \uparrow g\) of non-negative simple functions. Since \(f \geq 0\), the sequence \(g_n f \uparrow g f\) is also increasing pointwise. Apply MCT on both sides: \[ \begin{align*} \int_\Omega g \, d\nu &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_\Omega g_n \, d\nu \\\\ &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_\Omega g_n \, f \, d\mu \\\\ &= \int_\Omega g \, f \, d\mu, \end{align*} \] where the middle equality is Step 2 applied to each \(g_n\). This proves (2) for non-negative \(g\).

Step 4: Real-valued case.
For measurable \(g : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\), apply Step 3 separately to \(g^+\) and \(g^-\) and subtract. Both \(\int g^+ \, d\nu = \int g^+ f \, d\mu\) and \(\int g^- \, d\nu = \int g^- f \, d\mu\) hold. The integrability hypothesis \(g \in L^1(\nu)\), that is, \(\int |g| d\nu \lt \infty\), makes both finite by the non-negative identity applied to \(|g|\). The subtraction then yields \[ \int_\Omega g \, d\nu = \int_\Omega g \, f \, d\mu, \] with both sides finite. The integrability equivalence \(g \in L^1(\nu) \iff g f \in L^1(\mu)\) follows by applying the non-negative identity to \(|g|\).

Proof of (1).
Set \(f = d\nu/d\mu\) and \(k = d\mu/d\lambda\). For any \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), apply (2) with \(\nu \ll \mu\) and the test function \(g = \mathbf{1}_A\), then apply (2) again with \(\mu \ll \lambda\) and the test function \(g' = \mathbf{1}_A f\) (non-negative and measurable): \[ \begin{align*} \nu(A) &\stackrel{\text{(2)}_{\nu \ll \mu}}{=} \int_\Omega \mathbf{1}_A f \, d\mu \\\\ &\stackrel{\text{(2)}_{\mu \ll \lambda}}{=} \int_\Omega \mathbf{1}_A f \, k \, d\lambda \\\\ &= \int_A f k \, d\lambda. \end{align*} \] Hence \(\nu(A) = \int_A (f k) \, d\lambda\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\).

The product \(fk\) is measurable and non-negative. The value of \(f\) on \(\mu\)-null sets is undetermined, and such sets need not be \(\lambda\)-null, but the integral against \(\lambda\) is unaffected by this ambiguity. For any two representatives \(f, f'\) of \(d\nu/d\mu\), the set \(D = \{f \neq f'\}\) is \(\mu\)-null, so (2) with \(\mu \ll \lambda\), applied to \(\mathbf{1}_D\), gives \(\int_D k \, d\lambda = \mu(D) = 0\). Since \(k \geq 0\), this forces \(k = 0\) \(\lambda\)-a.e. on \(D\), and hence \(fk = f'k\) holds \(\lambda\)-a.e. Thus \(\int_A fk \, d\lambda = \nu(A)\) is well-defined regardless of the choice of representatives, exhibiting \(fk\) as a density of \(\nu\) with respect to \(\lambda\). By uniqueness of the Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to \(\lambda\), \(d\nu/d\lambda = fk = (d\nu/d\mu)(d\mu/d\lambda)\) holds \(\lambda\)-a.e.

The relation \(\nu \ll \lambda\) asserted in (1), which makes \(d\nu/d\lambda\) meaningful, is itself a consequence of \(\nu \ll \mu \ll \lambda\), since \(\lambda(A) = 0\) implies \(\mu(A) = 0\), which in turn implies \(\nu(A) = 0\).

Two notational conveniences are worth noting. First, the change-of-variables formula (2) is often written in the suggestive Leibniz form \[ \int g \, d\nu = \int g \, \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} \, d\mu, \] in which "\(d\nu = (d\nu/d\mu) \, d\mu\)" reads as a formal cancellation of differentials. This form underlies the reweighting identity of importance sampling, collected at the end of this section.

Second, the chain rule (1) gives the Radon-Nikodym derivative the algebraic structure of a classical derivative under composition. Transitivity of \(\ll\) at the level of measures lifts to multiplicativity of \(d/d\) at the level of densities.

A subtlety distinguishes this change-of-variables formula from the pushforward Change of Variables for random variables. The pushforward identity \(\int g \, dP_X = \int (g \circ X) \, d\mathbb{P}\) transports integrals across a measurable map between two different measurable spaces. The function \(g\) and its lift \(g \circ X\) live on different spaces, and the derivative \(dP_X/d\mathbb{P}\) is not even defined, because the two measures are defined on different \(\sigma\)-algebras.

The Radon-Nikodym change-of-variables, by contrast, transports integrals between two measures on the same space, paid for by multiplication by the density. Both are change-of-variables formulas, but they act in complementary directions. Pushforward changes the carrier space, while Radon-Nikodym changes the weighting.

Connection to Machine Learning

Many probabilistic quantities in modern machine learning are, at the foundational level, Radon-Nikodym derivatives, and many algorithms involving densities can be read as instances of the change-of-variables formula (2). The qualification "at the foundational level" matters. The rigorous Radon-Nikodym object licenses the construction, but the implementation typically works with explicit densities under a chosen reference measure, with neural-network surrogates, or with sample-average approximations rather than computing the abstract derivative directly. We collect four examples that span supervised, generative, and reinforcement learning, noting for each how the implementation relates to the underlying Radon-Nikodym structure.

Bayesian posterior density.
Let \(\Pi\) be a prior on the parameter space \(\Theta\), and let \(p(x \mid \theta)\) be a likelihood, viewed as a density of the conditional data distribution with respect to a reference measure on the data space. The Bayesian posterior \(\Pi_{\theta \mid x}\) is then defined as a measure on \(\Theta\), not a priori as a density. When \(\Pi \ll \lambda_\Theta\) (with \(\lambda_\Theta\) a reference measure on \(\Theta\), often Lebesgue measure), Bayes' rule produces the posterior density \(d\Pi_{\theta \mid x}/d\lambda_\Theta\) by the formula \[ \frac{d\Pi_{\theta \mid x}}{d\lambda_\Theta}(\theta) = \frac{p(x \mid \theta) \cdot (d\Pi/d\lambda_\Theta)(\theta)}{\int_\Theta p(x \mid \theta') \, (d\Pi/d\lambda_\Theta)(\theta') \, d\lambda_\Theta(\theta')}. \] If the prior is supported on a discrete set, \(\Pi \perp \lambda_\Theta\) and no Lebesgue density exists. The posterior is then a discrete measure, and its density relative to counting measure on the support plays the analogous role. The framework is unified by the choice of dominating measure.

Score function in diffusion models.
Score-based generative models, including denoising diffusion probabilistic models and score-based stochastic differential equations, train a neural network \(s_\phi\) to estimate the gradient of the log-density. This gradient is the Stein score, often simply called the "score" in the diffusion-modeling literature, and the network is trained so that \[ s_\phi(x, t) \approx \nabla_x \log p_t(x) = \nabla_x \log \frac{dP_t}{d\lambda}(x), \] where \(P_t\) is the distribution of the noised data at time \(t\) and \(\lambda\) is Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). The density \(dP_t/d\lambda\) exists when \(P_t \ll \lambda\), and in the diffusion-model setting it is smooth and strictly positive for \(t \gt 0\) thanks to the Gaussian noise injection along the forward diffusion, so the score is well-defined. This noise injection ensures the absolute continuity even when the original data distribution \(P_0\) lies on a low-dimensional manifold and is singular to \(\lambda\). The reverse-time generative SDE is then driven by \(s_\phi\), which stands in for \(\nabla_x \log (dP_t/d\lambda)\) in the dynamics. The implementation never computes the Radon-Nikodym derivative itself. It learns the gradient of its logarithm from data via score matching.

Importance sampling.
A direct application of the change-of-variables formula is importance sampling. To estimate \(\mathbb{E}_P[g(X)]\) using samples drawn from a different distribution \(Q\), one rewrites \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}_P[g(X)] &= \int g \, dP \\\\ &= \int g \cdot \frac{dP}{dQ} \, dQ \\\\ &= \mathbb{E}_Q\!\left[g(X) \cdot \frac{dP}{dQ}(X)\right] \end{align*} \] and replaces the expectation under \(P\) by the empirical mean under \(Q\) of the reweighted integrand \(g(X) \cdot dP/dQ(X)\). This is precisely the change-of-variables proposition with \(\nu = P\) and \(\mu = Q\). The estimator is unbiased whenever \(P \ll Q\) and \(g \in L^1(P)\), the latter ensuring that both expectations are finite. When \(P \not\ll Q\), the ratio \(dP/dQ\) is undefined on a set of positive \(P\)-measure, and the estimator misses contributions from that region. This is how a violation of absolute continuity shows up in practice.

KL divergence and RLHF regularization.
The Kullback-Leibler divergence between two probability measures \(P\) and \(Q\) is defined by \[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle \int_\Omega \log \frac{dP}{dQ} \, dP & \text{if } P \ll Q, \\ +\infty & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \] The divergence integrates the logarithm of the Radon-Nikodym derivative \(dP/dQ\). Without absolute continuity, \(dP/dQ\) does not exist as a Radon-Nikodym derivative on the portion of the space where \(Q\) places no mass but \(P\) does, and \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q)\) is set to \(+\infty\) by convention. In reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), the regularizer \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\) penalizes the trained policy \(\pi_\theta\) for drifting from a reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). It can be finite only when \(\pi_\theta \ll \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). In practice the divergence is estimated by the sample average of \(\log(\pi_\theta(y \mid x) / \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x))\) over sampled completions. The absolute-continuity hypothesis is a structural property of the model parameterization rather than an explicit runtime check. For example, a softmax over a shared vocabulary makes both policies positive on the same token set.

Looking Ahead

With Radon-Nikodym established, the earlier debts are settled at once. The probability density \(f_X = dP_X/d\lambda\) of a pushforward measure \(P_X\) is no longer a primitive but a derived object whose existence is guaranteed whenever \(P_X \ll \lambda\). The converse direction of \((L^p)^* \cong L^q\), stated in Dual Spaces & Riesz Representation and sketched in \(L^p\) Spaces for \(1 \leq p \lt \infty\) and \(\sigma\)-finite \(\mu\), now rests on a proved theorem. That sketch evaluates a continuous functional on \(L^p(\mu)\) at indicators of sets of finite measure to obtain a signed measure \(\nu \ll \mu\), and the representing \(L^q\) function is \(d\nu/d\mu\). And the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) previewed in Limit Theorems & Product Measures is now licensed for full construction.

The Lebesgue Decomposition

A natural companion to Radon-Nikodym states that absolute continuity and mutual singularity together exhaust the qualitative ways two measures can interact. Given a \(\sigma\)-finite non-negative measure \(\mu\) and a \(\sigma\)-finite signed measure \(\nu\), there exist unique signed measures \(\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}, \nu_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(\nu = \nu_{\mathrm{ac}} + \nu_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\nu_{\mathrm{ac}} \ll \mu\), and \(\nu_{\mathrm{s}} \perp \mu\). We state this decomposition without proof. Combined with our theorem, this yields a complete structural classification. Every \(\sigma\)-finite signed \(\nu\) splits into a density part (an integral against \(\mu\)) and a singular part (concentrated where \(\mu\) is not), and nothing else.

Onward

The page Conditional Expectation constructs \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) for \(X \in L^1(\mathbb{P})\) as the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the signed measure \(A \mapsto \int_A X \, d\mathbb{P}\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{G})\) with respect to \(\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{G}}\), from which martingale theory and stochastic calculus open up. Beyond that, the same theorem reappears in several guises: as Girsanov's change of measure in path-space (underlying the mathematical theory of score-based diffusion models and risk-neutral pricing), as the ELBO/KL decomposition in variational inference, and as the Fisher-information metric on parametric statistical manifolds in information geometry. Each of these guises extends Radon-Nikodym in its own direction.
