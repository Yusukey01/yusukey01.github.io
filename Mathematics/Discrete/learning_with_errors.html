A Problem Built from Noise

The previous page built one-way functions and collision-resistant hashing on the difficulty of finding short vectors in a random lattice, and closed by naming what that construction could not reach: public-key encryption. The obstruction was structural. The function \(\mathbf{z} \mapsto \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\) is compressing on short inputs and, once \(\mathbf{A}\) has many columns, essentially surjective. A map of that shape is a hash, which destroys information rather than protecting it behind a trapdoor. This page introduces the problem that supplies the missing shape, the same \(q\)-ary geometry read through an injective map, made hard not by compression but by a controlled amount of noise.

Convention. Where the short-integer-solution problem multiplied its unknown vector on the right, as \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\), the problem of this page places its secret on the left, as \(\mathbf{s}^\top\mathbf{A}\). The change is not substantive. It reflects that the two problems are duals of one another, a relationship the next section makes exact. But the convention is worth fixing now, because it governs every formula below.

The Distribution

Fix a dimension \(n\) and a modulus \(q\), as before, together with an error distribution \(\chi\) over \(\mathbb{Z}\). Typically \(\chi\) is a discrete Gaussian of width parameter \(\alpha q\), so that its standard deviation is of order \(\alpha q\), for some \(\alpha \lt 1\), and the parameter \(\alpha\) is called the relative error rate. A secret vector \(\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\) is fixed once. The problem is presented not as a single equation but as a source of noisy linear measurements of that secret.

Definition: The LWE Distribution

For a secret \(\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\), the LWE distribution \(A_{\mathbf{s}, \chi}\) over \(\mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{Z}_q\) is sampled by choosing \(\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\) uniformly at random, drawing an error \(e \leftarrow \chi\), and outputting \[ (\mathbf{a}, b = \langle \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a} \rangle + e \bmod q). \]

The second coordinate of each sample is an inner product of the secret with a random vector, computed modulo \(q\) and then pushed off its true value by a small error. Without the error the samples would be exact linear equations, and about \(n\) of them would typically pin down \(\mathbf{s}\) by Gaussian elimination in polynomial time. This is the same collapse that made unbounded SIS trivial, here for an inhomogeneous linear system instead of a homogeneous one. The noise is the entire difficulty. It is small enough that the samples still carry information about \(\mathbf{s}\), yet large enough that no efficient procedure has been found to disentangle the secret from the error.

Two Problems, Search and Decision

From this one distribution grow two problems, and the distinction between them will matter for both the hardness reduction and the encryption scheme.

Definition: Search-LWE

Given \(m\) independent samples \((\mathbf{a}_i, b_i) \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{Z}_q\) drawn from \(A_{\mathbf{s}, \chi}\) for a uniformly random secret \(\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\) fixed across all samples, search-LWE asks to recover \(\mathbf{s}\).

Definition: Decision-LWE

Given \(m\) independent samples \((\mathbf{a}_i, b_i) \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{Z}_q\), where every sample is drawn either (1) from \(A_{\mathbf{s}, \chi}\) for a uniformly random \(\mathbf{s}\) fixed across all samples, or (2) from the uniform distribution on \(\mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{Z}_q\), decision-LWE asks to distinguish which of the two is the case, with non-negligible advantage.

Search asks to extract the secret. Decision asks only whether a secret is there at all, that is, whether the noisy samples are structured or merely random. An encryption scheme leans on the hardness of decision, since hiding a message amounts to making a ciphertext indistinguishable from noise.

As with the previous problem, the sample count \(m\) is of secondary importance, taken large enough that the secret is determined with high probability and otherwise left unspecified. For a prime modulus polynomial in \(n\), the two problems turn out to be equivalent up to a polynomial loss, a fact the hardness section returns to. For now it is enough that both are believed hard, and that the belief will shortly be anchored to the worst-case lattice problems of the previous pages.

The Dual of the Hash

The short-integer-solution problem was a shortest-vector problem on the \(q\)-ary lattice \(\mathcal{L}^\perp(\mathbf{A})\). Learning with errors is also a lattice problem, on a different lattice built from the same matrix. The relationship between the two lattices is exactly the relationship between a hash and the encryption it could not provide.

Definition: The LWE Lattice \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A})\)

For a matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n \times m}\), the associated LWE lattice is \[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A}) = \{\, \mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{s} : \mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}^n \,\} + q\mathbb{Z}^m, \] the set of all integer vectors congruent modulo \(q\) to some \(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{s}\). Here \(\mathbf{s}\) ranges over integer vectors, but only its residues modulo \(q\) matter. Changing \(\mathbf{s}\) by a multiple of \(q\) shifts \(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{s}\) by an element of \(q\mathbb{Z}^m\), which the summand \(q\mathbb{Z}^m\) absorbs. The set is therefore well defined, and \(\mathbf{s}\) may equally be read as an element of \(\mathbb{Z}_q^n\).

The connection to the samples is immediate. If we collect \(m\) LWE samples into a matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) whose columns are the \(\mathbf{a}_i\) and a vector \(\mathbf{b}\) whose entries are the \(b_i\), the defining relation becomes \[ \mathbf{b}^\top \equiv \mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{e}^\top \pmod q, \quad \mathbf{e} \leftarrow \chi^m. \] The noiseless part \(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{s}\) is a point of \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A})\), and the observed \(\mathbf{b}\) is that point displaced by the short error \(\mathbf{e}\). Recovering the secret amounts to recovering the lattice point closest to \(\mathbf{b}\), provided the error is small enough that exactly one lattice point is within reach. The identification also requires the map \(\mathbf{s} \mapsto \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{s} \bmod q\) to be injective, a condition that holds with high probability when \(m\) is large compared with \(n\).

An Average-Case Bounded-Distance Problem

This recovery is a decoding problem. The target \(\mathbf{b}\) is promised to lie close to \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A})\), within the error width. For the parameters of interest, that width lies below half the minimum distance \(\lambda_1\) of \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A})\), so that a single lattice point is unambiguously the nearest, and the task is to find that unique nearby lattice point.

That task is precisely bounded-distance decoding, now in an average-case form. The lattice \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A})\) is random rather than adversarial, and the target is generated by the LWE distribution. Search-LWE is average-case BDD on the family of LWE lattices, exactly as SIS was an average-case shortest-vector problem on the family of \(q\)-ary lattices. In the other case of decision-LWE, where the samples are uniform instead, the vector \(\mathbf{b}\) is far from every lattice point with high probability, and no nearby point exists to decode.

The Two Lattices Are Dual

The SIS lattice and the LWE lattice are built from the same \(\mathbf{A}\), and they are duals of one another up to a scale of \(q\): \[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A}) = q \cdot \mathcal{L}^\perp(\mathbf{A})^*. \] By definition, a vector \(\mathbf{y}\) lies in \(q \cdot \mathcal{L}^\perp(\mathbf{A})^*\) exactly when \(\mathbf{y}/q \in \mathcal{L}^\perp(\mathbf{A})^*\). The definition of the dual turns this into the condition that \(\langle \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{y}/q \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}\) for every \(\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{L}^\perp(\mathbf{A})\), that is, for every \(\mathbf{z}\) with \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} \bmod q\). That membership condition can equally be written \(\langle \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{y} \rangle \equiv 0 \pmod q\). Every vector congruent to some \(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{s}\) satisfies it, since \[ \langle \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{s} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{s} \rangle \equiv 0 \pmod q. \]

For prime \(q\) the converse is a dimension count in \(\mathbb{Z}_q^m\). The residues satisfying the condition form the orthogonal complement of \(\ker \mathbf{A}\), of dimension \(m - \dim \ker \mathbf{A} = \operatorname{rank} \mathbf{A}\), and they contain the row space of \(\mathbf{A}\), which has the same dimension, so the two coincide. For composite \(q\) the converse still holds, by linear algebra over \(\mathbb{Z}_q\) that we take for granted here. Either way, the vectors satisfying the condition are exactly those of \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A})\).

The dual lattice introduced in the geometry pages, developed there with no application in sight, is exactly the bridge between the two central problems of lattice cryptography.

Why One Hashes and the Other Encrypts

The duality is not a curiosity. It is the reason the two problems support different cryptography. SIS is concerned with the map \(\mathbf{z} \mapsto \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\), which sends a short vector to its syndrome. For large \(m\) the map is surjective, and being many-to-one on short inputs, it cannot be inverted to a unique input. That is the shape of a hash.

LWE is concerned with the map \(\mathbf{s} \mapsto \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{s}\), which embeds a secret into a higher-dimensional space, where noise then perturbs the image. Once \(m\) is large, this map is injective. An injective map admits, in principle, a unique preimage, and the noise is what hides it. A unique preimage that only a hidden key can recover is the shape of a trapdoor, and a trapdoor is what public-key encryption requires. The surjective side compresses and destroys. The injective side embeds and conceals. That is the whole difference, and the final section turns it into a scheme.

A Quantum Reduction

Learning with errors inherits the guarantee that made its predecessor trustworthy. It is hard on average because a worst-case lattice problem is hard. But the reduction that establishes this differs from the one for the short-integer-solution problem in a way that is not incidental. The difference is quantum. This is the point at which the thread left hanging by the classical pages is drawn tight: that lattices resist quantum attack, yet are not untouched by quantum computation.

Theorem: Worst-Case Hardness of LWE

Fix any \(m = \mathrm{poly}(n)\), any modulus \(q\), and a discretized Gaussian error distribution \(\chi\) of width parameter \(\alpha q \geq 2\sqrt{n}\) with \(0 \lt \alpha \lt 1\). If there is an efficient algorithm that solves decision-LWE, then there is an efficient quantum algorithm that solves \(\mathrm{GapSVP}_\gamma\) and \(\mathrm{SIVP}_\gamma\) on every \(n\)-dimensional lattice, for an approximation factor \(\gamma = \tilde{O}(n/\alpha)\).

As with the short-integer-solution problem, the exact modulus and sample count play no role in this reduction beyond the lower bound \(q \geq 2\sqrt{n}/\alpha\) and the requirement that \(m\) be polynomial. The strength of the conclusion is governed by the error rate \(\alpha\), through the factor \(\gamma = \tilde{O}(n/\alpha)\). Smaller noise means a smaller \(\alpha\), hence a larger \(\gamma\), which is a weaker guarantee. There is therefore a genuine tension. With too little noise the problem loses its worst-case backing, and with too much the samples cease to determine the secret. The reduction converts any algorithm solving LWE, classical or quantum, into a quantum algorithm for lattice problems. It runs in two steps, and the second is where the quantum Fourier transform re-enters the story.

The Two Steps

The reduction is iterative. It produces progressively narrower Gaussian samples over the input lattice \(\mathcal{L}\) until they are sharp enough to read off solutions to the lattice problems of the theorem. One iteration couples a classical step to a quantum one. The LWE oracle it calls is the search form, while the theorem above assumes only the decision form. The bootstrapping that converts one into the other is recorded right after the sketch below.

One iteration of the reduction

Step one, classical. Given an oracle for search-LWE and a supply of discrete Gaussian samples over \(\mathcal{L}\) of some width \(r\), one solves bounded-distance decoding on the dual lattice \(\mathcal{L}^*\), to within a distance \(d \approx \alpha q / r\). The Gaussian samples over \(\mathcal{L}\) are combined with the BDD target to manufacture properly distributed LWE samples. The secret the oracle then recovers is exactly what pins down the closest dual-lattice vector, which solves the decoding instance.

Step two, quantum. Given an oracle for bounded-distance decoding on \(\mathcal{L}^*\) to within distance \(d\), one generates discrete Gaussian samples over \(\mathcal{L}\) of a narrower width \(r' \approx \sqrt{n}/d\). This is the step with no known classical counterpart. The BDD oracle is used to prepare a quantum state. Computing the quantum Fourier transform on that state and measuring it yields a fresh Gaussian sample over \(\mathcal{L}\).

Substituting \(d \approx \alpha q / r\) into \(r' \approx \sqrt{n}/d\) gives \(r' \approx \sqrt{n}\, r / (\alpha q)\), so the condition \(\alpha q \geq 2\sqrt{n}\) makes \(r'\) at most about \(r/2\). The width produced by step two is smaller than the width step one consumed, by a constant factor.

Feeding \(r'\) back into step one and iterating drives the Gaussian width steadily down, until the samples over \(\mathcal{L}\) are narrow enough that \(\mathrm{SIVP}_\gamma\) and \(\mathrm{GapSVP}_\gamma\) fall out directly. The detailed accounting of the widths and the discrete-Gaussian machinery that makes each step precise are beyond the scope of this page. What matters here is the architecture, in which a classical decoding step alternates with a quantum sampling step, and the role of the quantum transform in the second.

Here is that bootstrapping. For a prime modulus of size polynomial in \(n\), search and decision agree, up to a polynomial loss in the sample count, and later work extends the hardness of the decision form to every modulus. An oracle that merely distinguishes LWE samples from uniform can be bootstrapped, one coordinate of the secret at a time, into one that recovers the whole secret. That is the search oracle the reduction called for. The reverse direction is immediate, since a search oracle can distinguish by checking whether the residuals \(b_i - \langle \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}_i \rangle\) of its answer are small. The encryption scheme of the next section needs the decision form, which the theorem above addresses directly.

Why the Reduction Is Quantum, and What That Means

The quantum step is not a mere convenience. There is no known classical procedure that generates the narrower Gaussian samples from a BDD oracle, and the quantum Fourier transform is, so far, essential to that construction. This has a precise and initially unsettling consequence. The theorem guarantees only that breaking LWE yields a quantum algorithm for lattice problems. Hence a classical algorithm breaking LWE would still imply merely a quantum lattice algorithm, not a classical one.

Later work removes the quantum step in part, at a cost that sharpens the picture rather than dissolving it. A first classical reduction reached \(\mathrm{GapSVP}_\gamma\) but only for an exponentially large modulus, \(q \geq 2^{n/2}\). That barrier was later broken by a fully classical reduction for a modulus polynomial in \(n\), the regime of practical parameters. Two costs survive. The reduction is not known to reach \(\mathrm{SIVP}_\gamma\), only \(\mathrm{GapSVP}_\gamma\). And it loses dimension. Breaking \(n\)-dimensional LWE classically yields a lattice algorithm only in dimension \(\sqrt{n}\), so to underwrite a lattice problem of a given size, the classical route demands an LWE instance quadratically larger than the quantum route does.

The first cost traces to a structural fact that the proof above has already exhibited. The quantum reduction is iterative. Step two hands a narrower Gaussian back to step one, and the width descends by a constant factor each round. The classical route rests on a reduction that makes a single pass and so never produces the narrow Gaussian samples from which short independent vectors are read off. No such one-shot argument is known to reach \(\mathrm{SIVP}_\gamma\), which the iterative one handles. The second cost has a different source. The classical reduction first reaches LWE with an exponentially large modulus, and it brings the modulus down to a polynomial one by enlarging the dimension, which is where the quadratic loss enters.

Whether the loss is inherent remains open. The question is whether \(n\)-dimensional LWE with polynomial modulus is classically as hard as \(n\)-dimensional lattice problems, rather than merely \(\sqrt{n}\)-dimensional ones. The strongest known evidence for LWE's hardness still passes through a quantum computation. On the defensive side, this is the exact counterpart of the fact that the classical schemes fell to one.

Math \(\leftrightarrow\) Quantum: The Transform That Builds Instead of Breaks

The classical assumptions fell because the quantum Fourier transform detects the hidden abelian period inside factoring and the discrete logarithm. Lattice problems exhibit no such period, so that particular attack has nothing to grip. Yet the transform reappears here on the constructive side. It is the engine of the sampling step that certifies LWE's worst-case hardness. The security of lattice cryptography does not come from lattices being untouched by quantum computation. The honest statement is narrower. No efficient quantum algorithm for the underlying lattice problems is known, and the abelian structure that the transform exploits to break the classical schemes is simply absent. The same instrument that ends one cryptography helps certify the foundation of its successor.

The Cryptosystem

The opening of this page promised that the injective, noisy shape of learning with errors would unlock public-key encryption. Here is the scheme that redeems the promise. Its security reduces to decision-LWE, and hence, through the quantum reduction of the previous section, to the worst-case hardness of lattice problems.

Encrypting a Bit

The secret key is a random vector \(\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\). The public key is a collection of LWE samples \((\mathbf{a}_i, b_i)\) for that secret. They are noisy linear measurements, published in the open. Because the samples are LWE-distributed, the hardness of decision-LWE is exactly the assumption that they look like uniform random pairs and so reveal nothing about \(\mathbf{s}\).

To encrypt a single bit, one adds together a random subset of the public samples, obtaining a fresh pair \((\mathbf{a}, b)\) that is itself close to a valid LWE sample. The message bit is hidden in the last coordinate. To send a zero, leave \(b\) as it is. To send a one, shift it by \(\lfloor q/2 \rfloor\). The quantity \(b - \langle \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a} \rangle \bmod q\), which is the inner product of the ciphertext with \((-\mathbf{s}, 1)\), is close to \(0\) modulo \(q\) when the ciphertext encodes zero and close to \(q/2\) when it encodes one.

Holding the secret \(\mathbf{s}\), the receiver computes the inner product that tests which case holds and recovers the bit. Decryption succeeds as long as the error accumulated by summing the subset stays below \(q/4\), half the gap between the two cases. On the rare occasions it exceeds that threshold, the bit flips and decryption fails. This is the practical face of the tension named in the hardness section. Too little noise forfeits the worst-case guarantee, and too much invites decryption failure. Controlling its probability is a central concern in choosing the parameters of practical schemes.

Why It Is Secure

Semantic security follows from a single thought experiment. Imagine the public key were malformed, replaced by genuinely uniform random pairs with no underlying secret at all. The decision-LWE assumption says that no efficient adversary can tell a malformed key from a real one, so the two are computationally indistinguishable.

Under a malformed key, however, encryption is lossy. Provided the public key holds somewhat more than \((n+1)\log_2 q\) pairs, a ciphertext formed from uniform pairs is, up to negligible statistical error, independent of the message bit. This is a fact about random subset sums, stated here without proof. An adversary facing a malformed key therefore gains no information about the bit, not because the bit is computationally hidden, but because it is statistically absent from the ciphertext. Since the real key is indistinguishable from the malformed one, the adversary can do at most negligibly better against the real scheme. Any advantage in guessing the message would be an advantage in solving decision-LWE, which is assumed hard.

Math \(\leftrightarrow\) CS: Worst-Case Security, Deployed

Two properties set this construction apart from the number-theoretic schemes it is built to outlast. Its security rests on the worst-case hardness of general lattice problems, namely \(\mathrm{GapSVP}_\gamma\) and \(\mathrm{SIVP}_\gamma\), rather than on an average-case assumption about a chosen distribution, so no class of weak keys has to be avoided. And the underlying problems carry no abelian periodic structure for a quantum computer to seize, so the scheme is conjectured to resist the attack that felled the classical constructions. That is the same conjecture as before, that no efficient algorithm, classical or quantum, solves these lattice problems, but it now sits under an encryption scheme rather than a hash.

The word general in the first of those is doing work. Worst-case lattice security for public-key encryption was achieved earlier, but only for a restricted class, and by a classical reduction. That class consists of lattices promised to hold one vector far shorter than any other. Against that earlier guarantee this one is incomparable rather than strictly stronger. It reaches the general lattice, and the only reduction known to reach \(\mathrm{SIVP}_\gamma\) there is the quantum one.

From the Textbook Scheme to a Deployed Standard

The scheme above is deliberately minimal, with a single bit per ciphertext and keys quadratic in the dimension, and a long line of refinements separates it from what runs in practice. The efficiency gap is closed largely by working over a ring. Replacing the vectors of \(\mathbb{Z}_q^n\) with polynomials in a quotient ring endows the samples with algebraic structure that shrinks keys and speeds arithmetic, at the price of a more specialized hardness assumption. The structured variant that results is ring learning with errors, and its intermediate generalization, module learning with errors, is the foundation of the key-encapsulation mechanism standardized in 2024 as the primary post-quantum replacement for the classical key exchanges. That standard is the endpoint toward which the cryptography pages have been building. The algebraic structure and the ring geometry beneath it are the subject of the pages that follow.

It is worth marking what has been assembled. The classical pages built encryption on factoring and the discrete logarithm, then watched a quantum algorithm collapse the hidden abelian structure they depended on. The geometry pages developed minima, determinants, duals, and Minkowski's bound as pure objects. The complexity page turned that geometry into a hierarchy of hard problems. These two problems, the short-integer-solution problem and learning with errors, are where the threads converge, a hash and an encryption dual to one another. Both are grounded by reduction in the worst-case hardness of the very lattice problems the geometry pages measured, and both stand where the classical schemes fell. The foreshadowing is discharged.
