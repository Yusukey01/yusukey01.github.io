Vector Operators

The Clebsch-Gordan decomposition tells us how a tensor product of irreducibles breaks apart. The Wigner-Eckart theorem is the payoff. It says that once the irreducible content is known, the way one set of operators relates to another is forced to be a single number. The objects on which this rigidity acts are vector operators. Each is a triple of operators that transform under rotations the way the three coordinates of a vector do. We begin by making that phrase precise.

How Rotations Act on Operators

Fix a representation \(\Pi\) of \(SO(3)\) on a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\), and let \(\operatorname{End}(V)\) be the space of linear operators \(C : V \to V\). A rotation \(R\) already acts on the vectors of \(V\) through \(\Pi(R)\). It acts on an operator \(C\) by transporting the operator along with the space, \[ R \cdot C = \Pi(R)\,C\,\Pi(R)^{-1}. \] The reading is concrete. To apply the rotated operator to a vector, we first undo the rotation with \(\Pi(R)^{-1}\), apply \(C\) in the original frame, and then rotate the result back with \(\Pi(R)\).

This is the same conjugation pattern by which a change of basis acts on a matrix. A direct check using \(\Pi(R_1 R_2) = \Pi(R_1)\Pi(R_2)\) shows it is itself a representation of \(SO(3)\), now on the space \(\operatorname{End}(V)\): \[ R_1 \cdot (R_2 \cdot C) = \Pi(R_1)\Pi(R_2)\,C\,\Pi(R_2)^{-1}\Pi(R_1)^{-1} = (R_1 R_2) \cdot C. \] The map \(R \mapsto (C \mapsto R \cdot C)\) is smooth, its entries being polynomials in those of \(\Pi(R)\) and \(\Pi(R)^{-1}\), so the conjugation action is a representation in the sense used throughout.

The Defining Equivariance

Alongside this action on operators sits the most familiar representation of \(SO(3)\) of all: the rotation of ordinary vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), where \(R\) sends \(\mathbf{v}\) to \(R\mathbf{v}\). A vector operator is a triple \(\mathbf{C} = (C_1, C_2, C_3)\) of operators on \(V\) whose three components rotate among themselves in lockstep with the coordinate directions. To state this cleanly, assemble the triple into a single operator depending linearly on a direction \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3\), \[ \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{C} = \sum_{j=1}^{3} v_j\, C_j. \]

Definition: Vector Operator

Let \(V\) be a complex vector space, of any dimension, on which \(SO(3)\) acts by a group homomorphism \(\Pi\) into the invertible linear operators on \(V\). When \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(\Pi\) is smooth, this is a representation of \(SO(3)\). A triple \(\mathbf{C} = (C_1, C_2, C_3)\) of operators on \(V\) is a vector operator if \[ (R\mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{C} = \Pi(R)\,(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{C})\,\Pi(R)^{-1} \quad \text{for all } R \in SO(3) \text{ and all } \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3. \]

Unwinding the definition shows exactly what it demands. The right-hand side is the action on operators applied to \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{C}\). The left-hand side first rotates the direction \(\mathbf{v}\) and then forms the combination. The equation says that rotating the direction and rotating the operator are the same thing. In the language of the intertwining maps of representations, the linear map \(\mathbf{v} \mapsto \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{C}\) from \(\mathbb{R}^3\) into \(\operatorname{End}(V)\) intertwines the rotation action on \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with the conjugation action on \(\operatorname{End}(V)\). A vector operator is precisely such an intertwiner. This single observation is what lets the representation theory take over.

A concrete instance fixes the idea. Let \(V\) be the space of polynomial functions on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), on which \(SO(3)\) acts by rotating the argument, \((\Pi(R) f)(\mathbf{x}) = f(R^{-1}\mathbf{x})\), and let \(X_j\) be multiplication by the \(j\)-th coordinate, \((X_j f)(\mathbf{x}) = x_j\, f(\mathbf{x})\). Both operations carry polynomials to polynomials. The triple \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2, X_3)\), the position operators of physics, is a vector operator. Rotating the coordinate that one multiplies by is the same as conjugating the multiplication operator by the rotation. Since \((R\mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v} \cdot (R^{-1}\mathbf{x})\) for \(R \in SO(3)\), \[ \begin{align*} \bigl(((R\mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{X})\, f\bigr)(\mathbf{x}) &= ((R\mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{x})\, f(\mathbf{x}) \\\\ &= (\mathbf{v} \cdot R^{-1}\mathbf{x})\, f(\mathbf{x}), \\\\ \bigl(\Pi(R)\,(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{X})\,\Pi(R)^{-1} f\bigr)(\mathbf{x}) &= \bigl((\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{X})\,\Pi(R^{-1}) f\bigr)(R^{-1}\mathbf{x}) \\\\ &= (\mathbf{v} \cdot R^{-1}\mathbf{x})\, f(\mathbf{x}), \end{align*} \] which is the defining identity above.

The Infinitesimal Form

Differentiating the defining identity at the identity rotation converts it into a bracket condition on the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\), often the more practical form. Let \(V\) be finite-dimensional, let \(\pi\) be the Lie algebra representation associated to \(\Pi\), and take the basis \(E_1, E_2, E_3\) of \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\) with \([E_j, E_k] = \sum_{l} \varepsilon_{jkl} E_l\), where \(E_j\) is the hat map of \(\mathbf{e}_j\), so that \(E_j \mathbf{e}_k = \mathbf{e}_j \times \mathbf{e}_k = \sum_{l} \varepsilon_{jkl}\, \mathbf{e}_l\). The symbol \(\varepsilon_{jkl}\) is \(+1\) on a cyclic permutation of \((1,2,3)\), \(-1\) on a non-cyclic permutation, and \(0\) when an index repeats. Fix \(j\) and \(k\), and put \(R = e^{tE_j}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{e}_k\) in the defining identity. The left side is \((e^{tE_j}\mathbf{e}_k) \cdot \mathbf{C}\), with derivative \((E_j \mathbf{e}_k) \cdot \mathbf{C} = \sum_{l} \varepsilon_{jkl}\, C_l\) at \(t = 0\). The right side is \(\Pi(e^{tE_j})\, C_k\, \Pi(e^{tE_j})^{-1} = \Pi(e^{tE_j})\, C_k\, \Pi(e^{-tE_j})\), whose two factors have derivatives \(\pi(E_j)\) and \(-\pi(E_j)\) at \(t = 0\), so the product rule gives the derivative \(\pi(E_j)\, C_k - C_k\, \pi(E_j)\). Hence a vector operator satisfies \[ [\pi(E_j), C_k] = \sum_{l} \varepsilon_{jkl}\, C_l. \] Conversely, because \(SO(3)\) is connected, this bracket condition implies the defining identity. We state the converse without proof and do not use it.

The components \(C_k\) rotate under the infinitesimal generators exactly as the basis vectors of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) rotate under the cross product. The same structure constants \(\varepsilon_{jkl}\) appear on both sides, which is the algebraic shadow of the correspondence between the cross product and the bracket on \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\).

Endomorphisms as a Representation

The proof of the Wigner-Eckart theorem turns the geometric statement about vector operators into an algebraic one about a representation, and then lets Schur's lemma finish the job. The bridge is an identification of the operator space \(\operatorname{End}(V)\) with a tensor product, plus one special feature of the rotation group. Its irreducibles are their own duals. We establish both here.

Operators as a Tensor Product

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional representation of \(SO(3)\) and \(V^*\) its dual representation. There is a natural way to build an operator on \(V\) out of a covector and a vector. Given \(\varphi \in V^*\) and \(v \in V\), the rule \[ w \longmapsto \varphi(w)\, v \] is a linear operator on \(V\). It reads off the \(\varphi\)-component of its input and returns that multiple of \(v\).

This assignment \((\varphi, v) \mapsto \varphi(\,\cdot\,)v\) is bilinear, so by the universal property of the tensor product it factors through a unique linear map \[ \Psi : V^* \otimes V \longrightarrow \operatorname{End}(V), \quad \Psi(\varphi \otimes v)(w) = \varphi(w)\, v. \] The universal property is stated for real spaces, but its proof uses only the vector space operations and so applies verbatim over \(\mathbb{C}\).

Proposition (Endomorphisms as a Tensor Product)

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional representation of \(SO(3)\). The map \(\Psi\) above is an isomorphism of representations \[ \operatorname{End}(V) \cong V^* \otimes V, \] where \(SO(3)\) acts on \(\operatorname{End}(V)\) by conjugation, \(R \cdot C = \Pi(R)\,C\,\Pi(R)^{-1}\), and on \(V^* \otimes V\) by the tensor product representation \(\Pi^* \otimes \Pi\).

Proof:

First, \(\Psi\) is a vector-space isomorphism. Pick a basis \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) of \(V\) with dual basis \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n\) of \(V^*\), so that \(\varphi_i(v_k) = \delta_{ik}\). Then \(\Psi(\varphi_i \otimes v_j)\) is the operator sending \(v_k\) to \(\delta_{ik} v_j\). This is the elementary matrix \(E_{ji}\), with a \(1\) in row \(j\) and column \(i\). As \((i, j)\) ranges over all pairs, these are exactly the \(n^2\) elementary matrices, a basis of \(\operatorname{End}(V)\). So \(\Psi\) carries a basis of \(V^* \otimes V\) to a basis of \(\operatorname{End}(V)\) and is an isomorphism of vector spaces.

Next, \(\Psi\) intertwines the two actions. On the tensor product, a rotation acts by \((\Pi^* \otimes \Pi)(R) = \Pi^*(R) \otimes \Pi(R)\) with \(\Pi^*(R) = [\Pi(R^{-1})]^\top\). Applying \(\Psi\) to a rotated elementary tensor and evaluating on \(w \in V\), we obtain \[ \begin{align*} \Psi\big((\Pi^*(R)\varphi) \otimes (\Pi(R) v)\big)(w) &= (\Pi^*(R)\varphi)(w)\, \Pi(R) v \\\\ &= \varphi\big(\Pi(R)^{-1} w\big)\, \Pi(R) v \\\\ &= \Pi(R)\,\big[\varphi(\Pi(R)^{-1} w)\, v\big] \\\\ &= \Pi(R)\,\Psi(\varphi \otimes v)\big(\Pi(R)^{-1} w\big) \\\\ &= \big(\Pi(R)\,\Psi(\varphi \otimes v)\,\Pi(R)^{-1}\big)(w). \end{align*} \] The second line uses the definition of the dual action, namely that \((\Pi^*(R)\varphi)(w) = \varphi(\Pi(R)^{-1} w)\). The third uses linearity of \(\Pi(R)\) to move the scalar \(\varphi(\Pi(R)^{-1} w)\) inside.

The result is the conjugation action on \(\operatorname{End}(V)\) applied to \(\Psi(\varphi \otimes v)\). Thus \(\Psi\) intertwines, and being an invertible intertwiner, it is an isomorphism of representations.

The Irreducibles Are Self-Dual

The second ingredient is special to the rotation group. We first record which representations of \(SO(3)\) are irreducible, in the labelling of the previous pages. There \(V_m\) denotes the irreducible representation of \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\) of dimension \(m + 1\), \(\sigma_m\) the representation of \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\) obtained from it through the isomorphism \(\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3)\), and \(\Sigma_m\), for even \(m\), the representation of \(SO(3)\) supplied by the lifting criterion.

Proposition (Irreducible Representations of \(SO(3)\))

Every irreducible finite-dimensional complex representation \(V\) of \(SO(3)\) is isomorphic to \(\Sigma_m\) for exactly one even integer \(m \geq 0\), namely \(m = \dim V - 1\), and each \(\Sigma_m\) with \(m\) even is irreducible. In particular two irreducible representations of \(SO(3)\) of the same dimension are isomorphic, and the dimensions that occur are exactly the odd numbers \(2\ell + 1\), \(\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots\).

Proof:

Let \((\Pi, V)\) be an irreducible representation of \(SO(3)\) and \(\sigma\) the associated representation of \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\), so that \(\Pi(e^{X}) = e^{\sigma(X)}\). The group \(SO(3)\) is connected, so \(\sigma\) is irreducible by the group-algebra correspondence (Point 1). The irreducible representations of \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\) are, up to isomorphism, the \(\sigma_m\), one in each dimension \(m + 1\), so there is an isomorphism \(T : V \to V_m\) with \(T\,\sigma(X) = \sigma_m(X)\,T\) for all \(X \in \mathfrak{so}(3)\), and \(m = \dim V - 1\). Then \(R \mapsto T\,\Pi(R)\,T^{-1}\) is a representation of \(SO(3)\) on \(V_m\) satisfying \(T\,\Pi(e^{X})\,T^{-1} = T e^{\sigma(X)} T^{-1} = e^{\sigma_m(X)}\), by property (e) of the matrix exponential in any basis, so its associated representation is \(\sigma_m\). The lifting criterion excludes odd \(m\). Conversely, for even \(m\) the lifting criterion provides \(\Sigma_m\) with associated representation \(\sigma_m\), which is irreducible, so \(\Sigma_m\) is irreducible by the correspondence (Point 1), and \(\dim \Sigma_m = m + 1\) is odd. Finally, if two irreducible representations of \(SO(3)\) have the same dimension \(m + 1\), their associated representations are both isomorphic to \(\sigma_m\), hence to each other, and the correspondence (Point 2) makes the representations themselves isomorphic.

Intertwiners pass from the group to the Lie algebra. Let \(T : V \to W\) be an intertwining map of two finite-dimensional representations \(\Pi\) and \(\Sigma\) of \(SO(3)\). Differentiating \(T\,\Pi(e^{tX}) = \Sigma(e^{tX})\,T\) at \(t = 0\) gives \(T\,\pi(X) = \sigma(X)\,T\) for the associated representations, and since \(T\) is complex-linear the same identity holds for the complex-linear extensions of \(\pi\) and \(\sigma\) to \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\), transported through \(\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3)\). The hypothesis of that extension, \(\mathfrak{su}(2) \cap i\,\mathfrak{su}(2) = \{0\}\), holds because a matrix that is both skew-Hermitian and Hermitian is zero. An isomorphism of \(SO(3)\)-representations is therefore also an isomorphism of the corresponding \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\)-representations, and by the proposition an irreducible representation of \(SO(3)\) of dimension \(m + 1\) is, as a representation of \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\), isomorphic to \(V_m\). This is the form in which the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of the previous page will be applied.

The three-dimensional case will matter most. The rotation of ordinary vectors, extended to \(\mathbb{C}^3\) by letting \(R\) act on complex vectors by the same matrix, is irreducible. Indeed, let \(W \subseteq \mathbb{C}^3\) be a nonzero invariant subspace. It is invariant under each \(e^{tE_j}\), hence under \(E_j\), the derivative at \(t = 0\), because \(W\) is closed. The generator \(E_3\) has the eigenvectors \(\mathbf{e}_1 + i\mathbf{e}_2\), \(\mathbf{e}_3\), and \(\mathbf{e}_1 - i\mathbf{e}_2\) with the distinct eigenvalues \(-i\), \(0\), and \(i\). Write a nonzero \(w \in W\) as \(w = w_{+} + w_0 + w_{-}\) along these eigenvectors. Then \((E_3^2 + I)\,w = w_0\), \(E_3(E_3 - iI)\,w = -2\,w_{+}\), and \(E_3(E_3 + iI)\,w = -2\,w_{-}\) all lie in \(W\), so \(W\) contains one of the three eigenvectors. If \(\mathbf{e}_3 \in W\), then \(E_2 \mathbf{e}_3 = \mathbf{e}_1\) and \(E_1 \mathbf{e}_3 = -\mathbf{e}_2\) lie in \(W\), so \(W = \mathbb{C}^3\). If \(\mathbf{e}_1 \pm i\mathbf{e}_2 \in W\), then \(E_1(\mathbf{e}_1 \pm i\mathbf{e}_2) = \pm i\,\mathbf{e}_3 \in W\), and we are back in the first case. By the proposition, \(\mathbb{C}^3\) is therefore isomorphic to \(\Sigma_2\), and as a representation of \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\) to \(V_2\).

Proposition (Self-Duality of the Irreducibles)

Every finite-dimensional irreducible representation \(V\) of \(SO(3)\) is isomorphic to its dual: \(V \cong V^*\).

Proof:

The dual \(V^*\) has the same dimension as \(V\), and passing to the dual representation preserves irreducibility, an invariant subspace of \(V^*\) being the annihilator of an invariant subspace of \(V\), of complementary dimension. Thus \(V\) and \(V^*\) are irreducible representations of \(SO(3)\) of the same dimension, hence isomorphic by the classification of irreducibles above.

Combining the two propositions gives the form in which the operator space enters the proof. For an irreducible \(V\), self-duality lets us replace \(V^*\) by \(V\). An isomorphism \(T : V^* \to V\) of representations gives the isomorphism \(T \otimes I : V^* \otimes V \to V \otimes V\), which intertwines the two tensor product representations. Hence \[ \operatorname{End}(V) \cong V^* \otimes V \cong V \otimes V. \] The right-hand side is a tensor product of two irreducibles, and its irreducible content is exactly what the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition computes. This is the lever. A question about operators has become a question about how the standard three-dimensional representation sits inside \(V \otimes V\), which the previous page already answered.

The Wigner-Eckart Theorem

Everything is now in place. On an irreducible representation, the operator space contains only one slot into which a vector operator can map, and Schur's lemma forces any two vector operators to fill that slot in proportion. The result is a rigidity statement of unusual economy. A single complex number relates one vector operator to another.

Theorem (Wigner-Eckart, Irreducible Case)

Let \((\Pi, V)\) be an irreducible finite-dimensional complex representation of \(SO(3)\), and let \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) be two vector operators on \(V\), with \(\mathbf{A}\) nonzero. Then there is a constant \(c \in \mathbb{C}\) such that \[ \mathbf{B} = c\, \mathbf{A}. \]

Before the proof, it is worth saying what the statement buys. A vector operator on an \(n\)-dimensional space is, on its face, three \(n \times n\) matrices, a great many numbers. The theorem says that once one nonzero vector operator \(\mathbf{A}\) is known on an irreducible \(V\), every other vector operator on \(V\) is a scalar multiple of it. All the apparent freedom collapses to one number.

Proof

Proof:

Recall that a vector operator is, by definition, an intertwining map \[ \phi_{\mathbf{A}} : \mathbb{R}^3 \longrightarrow \operatorname{End}(V), \quad \phi_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{A}, \] from the rotation action on \(\mathbb{R}^3\) to the conjugation action on \(\operatorname{End}(V)\), and likewise \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\) for \(\mathbf{B}\). Both extend complex-linearly to intertwiners out of the complexification \(\mathbb{C}^3\), which carries the three-dimensional irreducible representation \(\Sigma_2\) of \(SO(3)\), as shown in the previous section. By the same section, \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}\) and \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\) are then intertwiners of the associated representations of \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\), and it is there that we compare them. As representations of \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\), \(\mathbb{C}^3 \cong V_2\) and \(V \cong V_m\) with \(m + 1 = \dim V\), and the representation associated to \(\Pi \otimes \Pi\) is \(\pi \otimes \pi\), the outer-product formula evaluated at \((X, X)\). Tensoring an isomorphism \(V \to V_m\) with itself, and using \(\operatorname{End}(V) \cong V^* \otimes V \cong V \otimes V\) from the previous section, the last step by the self-duality of the irreducible \(V\), we obtain \(\operatorname{End}(V) \cong V_m \otimes V_m\) as representations of \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\).

The Clebsch-Gordan decomposition writes \(V_m \otimes V_m\) as \(V_{2m} \oplus V_{2m-2} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_0\), a direct sum of irreducible invariant subspaces \(N_1, \dots, N_r\) in which \(V_2\) occurs at most once. It occurs exactly once when \(m \geq 1\), and not at all when \(m = 0\), that is, when \(V\) is one-dimensional. This single occurrence is the multiplicity-freeness established on the previous page, read off the decomposition. Because each \(N_k\) is invariant, the projection \(p_k : \operatorname{End}(V) \to N_k\) along the other summands is an intertwiner, so \(p_k \circ \phi_{\mathbf{A}} : \mathbb{C}^3 \to N_k\) is an intertwiner between irreducibles. By the first part of Schur's lemma it is zero unless \(N_k \cong V_2\), and the same holds for \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\).

Suppose first that \(V\) is one-dimensional. Then no \(N_k\) is isomorphic to \(V_2\), so \(p_k \circ \phi_{\mathbf{A}} = 0\) for every \(k\), hence \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}} = 0\), contradicting that \(\mathbf{A}\) is nonzero. Hence \(m \geq 1\), and exactly one summand, call it \(U\), is isomorphic to \(V_2\).

The same projections show that \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}\) and \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\) land in \(U\), since every \(p_k\) with \(N_k \neq U\) annihilates both. Because \(\mathbf{A}\) is nonzero, \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}\) is a nonzero intertwiner \(\mathbb{C}^3 \to U\) between isomorphic irreducibles. If \(\mathbf{B} = 0\) the conclusion holds with \(c = 0\), so assume \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\) is nonzero as well. Applying the part of Schur's lemma comparing two intertwiners to the pair \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}, \phi_{\mathbf{A}} : \mathbb{C}^3 \to U\), we obtain a constant \(c \in \mathbb{C}\) with \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}} = c\, \phi_{\mathbf{A}}\). Evaluating on \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) gives \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{B} = c\,(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{A})\) for every direction, which is precisely \(\mathbf{B} = c\, \mathbf{A}\).

One Number for the Whole Operator

The force of the theorem is that the proportionality constant \(c\) is the only datum not fixed by symmetry. Two vector operators built from entirely different physical quantities, say a position and a momentum, must be scalar multiples of each other once they are restricted to an irreducible subspace. The multiple depends on the subspace but on nothing else. The geometry of rotation has used up every other degree of freedom. The next section extends this from a single irreducible to operators between two different irreducible pieces, where the same mechanism reappears as a rule about matrix entries.

Matrix Elements and Equivariance

The irreducible case assumed the whole space \(V\) was irreducible. In applications the interesting operators act on a large, reducible space and we care about how they connect two irreducible pieces of it. The general theorem handles this, and the conclusion is the one that organizes computation. Between any two fixed irreducible subspaces, the entries of every vector operator are the same up to a single constant.

From Endomorphisms to Homomorphisms

The bridge from the previous section generalizes from operators on one space to homomorphisms between two. If \(W_1\) and \(W_2\) are finite-dimensional representations of \(SO(3)\), the space \(\operatorname{Hom}(W_2, W_1)\) of linear maps \(W_2 \to W_1\) carries the conjugation-type action \(C \mapsto \Pi_1(R)\, C\, \Pi_2(R)^{-1}\), where \(\Pi_i\) is the action on \(W_i\). The same construction as before identifies it with a tensor product.

Proposition (Homomorphisms as a Tensor Product)

Let \(W_1\) and \(W_2\) be finite-dimensional representations of \(SO(3)\). Then there is an isomorphism of representations \[ \operatorname{Hom}(W_2, W_1) \cong W_2^* \otimes W_1. \] If in addition \(W_2\) is irreducible, hence self-dual, this becomes \(\operatorname{Hom}(W_2, W_1) \cong W_2 \otimes W_1\).

The proof repeats that of the endomorphism case verbatim, with the rule \(\Psi(\varphi \otimes w_1)(w_2) = \varphi(w_2)\, w_1\) for \(\varphi \in W_2^*\) and \(w_1 \in W_1\). Taking \(W_1 = W_2 = V\) recovers the endomorphism isomorphism.

The General Theorem

Theorem (Wigner-Eckart)

Let \(V\) be a complex inner product space, possibly infinite-dimensional, and let \(\Pi\) be a group homomorphism from \(SO(3)\) to the invertible linear operators on \(V\) that preserve the inner product. Let \(W_1\) and \(W_2\) be finite-dimensional invariant subspaces of \(V\) on which \(\Pi\) restricts to irreducible representations, and let \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) be vector operators on \(V\) for which \(\langle w, A_j w' \rangle\) is nonzero for some \(w \in W_1\), \(w' \in W_2\), and \(j \in \{1,2,3\}\). Then there is a constant \(c \in \mathbb{C}\) such that \[ \langle w, B_j w' \rangle = c\, \langle w, A_j w' \rangle \] for all \(w \in W_1\), all \(w' \in W_2\), and all \(j = 1, 2, 3\).

Proof:

The operators \(A_j\) need not carry \(W_2\) into \(W_1\), but taking the inner product of \(A_j w'\) against an element of \(W_1\) records only the \(W_1\)-component of \(A_j w'\). Let \(P_1 : V \to W_1\) be the orthogonal projection onto \(W_1\). It exists because \(W_1\) is finite-dimensional. Expanding along an orthonormal basis of \(W_1\), obtained from any basis by the Gram-Schmidt process, produces for each \(x \in V\) a vector \(P_1 x \in W_1\) with \(x - P_1 x\) orthogonal to \(W_1\). Since \(W_1\) is invariant and \(\Pi\) preserves the inner product, the orthogonal complement \(W_1^\perp\) is invariant too (for \(u \perp W_1\) and \(w \in W_1\), \(\langle \Pi(R) u, w \rangle = \langle u, \Pi(R)^{-1} w \rangle = 0\)), so \(P_1\) commutes with every \(\Pi(R)\). Define \[ \phi_{\mathbf{A}} : \mathbb{R}^3 \longrightarrow \operatorname{Hom}(W_2, W_1), \quad \phi_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{v})(w') = P_1\big((\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{A})\, w'\big), \] and likewise \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\). Because \(\mathbf{A}\) is a vector operator and \(P_1\) commutes with the action, for \(w' \in W_2\) \[ \begin{align*} \phi_{\mathbf{A}}(R\mathbf{v})(w') &= P_1\big(\Pi(R)\,(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{A})\,\Pi(R)^{-1} w'\big) \\\\ &= \Pi(R)\,\phi_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{v})\big(\Pi(R)^{-1} w'\big), \end{align*} \] so \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}\), extended complex-linearly as before, is an intertwining map from \(\mathbb{C}^3\) into \(\operatorname{Hom}(W_2, W_1)\).

Now \(\operatorname{Hom}(W_2, W_1) \cong W_2 \otimes W_1\) by the proposition above, and the same passage to \(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\) as in the irreducible case identifies \(W_2 \otimes W_1\) with \(V_a \otimes V_b\), where \(a + 1 = \dim W_2\) and \(b + 1 = \dim W_1\). The Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of \(V_a \otimes V_b\) contains \(V_2\) at most once, its summands having distinct dimensions. Projecting onto the summands as before, \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}\) and \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\) vanish on every summand not isomorphic to \(V_2\) by the first part of Schur's lemma. If \(V_2\) does not occur, both are zero, and all the inner products \(\langle w, A_j w'\rangle\) would then be zero, against the hypothesis. So \(V_2\) occurs exactly once, and both \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}\) and \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}}\) map into that single copy. If \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}} = 0\) the conclusion holds with \(c = 0\), so assume it is nonzero. Since \(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}\) is nonzero as well, comparison of two intertwiners gives a constant \(c\) with \(\phi_{\mathbf{B}} = c\, \phi_{\mathbf{A}}\).

Finally, the orthogonal projection \(P_1\) is self-adjoint and acts as the identity on \(W_1\), so for \(w \in W_1\), \[ \langle w,\, (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{A})\, w' \rangle = \langle w,\, P_1 (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{A})\, w' \rangle = \langle w,\, \phi_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{v})\, w' \rangle, \] and the same with \(\mathbf{A}\) replaced by \(\mathbf{B}\). Therefore \(\langle w, (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{B}) w' \rangle = c\, \langle w, (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{A}) w' \rangle\) for every \(\mathbf{v}\). Taking \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{e}_j\) yields the claim.

Reduced Matrix Elements and Weight Sharing

The constant \(c\) is what physics calls the reduced matrix element. Read as a statement about computation, the theorem describes the full array of numbers \(\langle w, A_j w' \rangle\) as \(w\), \(w'\), and \(j\) vary over a pair of irreducible subspaces. That array is determined by one scalar once the geometry-fixed part is known. The dependence on the indices is universal, the same for every vector operator between that pair of irreducibles. Only the overall scale carries information about the specific operator.

This is the principle a rotation-equivariant network exploits when it couples features. A layer mapping type-\(\ell_2\) features to type-\(\ell_1\) features through a vector quantity has, a priori, many weights. Equivariance forces those weights to follow the fixed index pattern and leaves a single free parameter for each pair of feature types. The reduction from a full weight matrix to one learnable scalar per irreducible pair is the parameter economy usually credited with the sample efficiency of equivariant models. Schur's lemma does the reducing, not an architectural choice.

The setting also accommodates the infinite-dimensional spaces of analysis. When \(V\) is a space of square-integrable functions on \(\mathbb{R}^3\) under the rotation action, its decomposition into finite-dimensional irreducible subspaces is the harmonic content of the sphere. The matrix entries of any vector operator between two such pieces again reduce to a single constant. Pursuing that decomposition leads to the theory of harmonic analysis on the rotation group, where the irreducibles classified here reappear as the building blocks of every square-integrable function.
